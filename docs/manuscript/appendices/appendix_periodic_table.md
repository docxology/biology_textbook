# Appendix E — A Periodic Table for Biology {#sec:appendix_periodic_table .unnumbered}



<!-- chapter-metadata-badge -->
> **Appendix E** · Level 1/3 · 15 min read · Prerequisites: \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}

Element requirements depend on the organism and its environment. This appendix summarizes bulk components, selected macro-minerals and trace elements, their biological roles, and examples of deficiency or excess; it is not a universal list of elements required by every species.

---

## E.1 The Six Bulk Elements (CHNOPS) {.unnumbered}

The rounded body-mass fractions listed below sum to 97.25%, or about 97%; they are illustrative human composition values, not a universal composition of living matter.

| Element | Symbol | ~% body mass | Where | Biological role |
| ------- | ------ | ------------ | ----- | --------------- |
| Oxygen | O | 65 % | water, organic molecules | Electron acceptor in respiration; H-bond acceptor; part of every major biomolecule |
| Carbon | C | 18 % | organic biomolecules, CO₂/HCO₃⁻ pools | Tetravalent backbone of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, and nucleic acids (see \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}) |
| Hydrogen | H | 10 % | water and most organic biomolecules | Partner in polar bonds; proton transfer; pH |
| Nitrogen | N | 3 % | amino acids, nucleic-acid bases, ATP | Amino and amide groups; ring atoms in bases, rather than the sugar-phosphate DNA/RNA backbone |
| Phosphorus | P | 1 % | DNA/RNA backbone, ATP, phospholipids | Phosphodiester bonds; free-energy carrier (ATP); membrane structure |
| Sulfur | S | 0.25 % | cysteine, methionine, CoA, Fe-S | Disulfide bridges in proteins; thioester bonds in metabolism |

## E.2 Essential Macro-Minerals (Electrolytes + Osteogenics) {.unnumbered}

| Element | Symbol | Common form | Role | Deficiency | Excess |
| ------- | ------ | ----------- | ---- | ---------- | ------ |
| Sodium | Na | Na⁺ | Principal extracellular cation; action potential upstroke | Hyponatraemia → seizures | Hypernatraemia → dehydration |
| Potassium | K | K⁺ | Principal intracellular cation; resting potential | Hypokalaemia → arrhythmia, muscle weakness | Hyperkalaemia → cardiac arrest |
| Calcium | Ca | Ca²⁺ | Bone matrix; signaling; coagulation; muscle contraction | Hypocalcaemia → tetany | Hypercalcaemia → stones, constipation |
| Magnesium | Mg | Mg²⁺ | ATP cofactor; enzyme cofactor; ribosome structure | Hypomagnesemia → arrhythmia, tetany | Oral excess can cause diarrhea; severe systemic excess can cause hypotension, respiratory depression, and cardiac arrest, especially with impaired renal clearance |
| Chloride | Cl | Cl⁻ | Major anion; gastric HCl; GABA-A/glycine receptors | Loss with vomiting | Acidosis |
| Phosphate | P | HPO₄²⁻ | Energy transfer (ATP); DNA backbone; bone mineralisation | Muscle weakness | Soft tissue calcification (CKD) |
| Sulfate | S | SO₄²⁻ | Sulfated proteoglycans; sulfation of xenobiotics | Rare isolated deficiency | Rare |

## E.3 Trace Elements (Enzyme Cofactors) {.unnumbered}

Requirements are element- and organism-specific. Established human trace-nutrient requirements are commonly expressed in mg day⁻¹ to μg day⁻¹, but a beneficial effect does not itself establish essentiality or a deficiency syndrome. Fluoride helps prevent caries; human chromium essentiality remains disputed, and no clinically defined chromium-deficiency state has been established (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, [Chromium: Health Professional Fact Sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Chromium-HealthProfessional/)) \citep{nihodschromium}.

| Element | Role | Deficiency or relevant low-exposure/handling disorder | Pathology of excess |
| ------- | ---- | ----------------------- | ------------------- |
| Iron (Fe) | Hemoglobin, cytochromes, Fe-S clusters | Anemia, fatigue | Hereditary haemochromatosis (iron overload) |
| Copper (Cu) | Cytochrome c oxidase, tyrosinase, lysyl oxidase | Menkes disease (curly hair, neurodegeneration) | Wilson disease (Cu deposition in liver, brain) |
| Zinc (Zn) | >300 enzymes; zinc finger transcription factors | Impaired growth, taste, immunity | Interferes with Cu absorption |
| Manganese (Mn) | Superoxide dismutase, glutamine synthetase | Rare; impaired connective tissue | Parkinson-like syndrome (welders) |
| Cobalt (Co) | Vitamin B₁₂ core | Megaloblastic anemia, neuropathy | Rare cardiomyopathy (beer cobalt in 1960s) |
| Molybdenum (Mo) | Xanthine oxidase, sulfite oxidase, nitrate reductase | Rare; sulfite oxidase deficiency | Gout-like syndrome |
| Selenium (Se) | Glutathione peroxidase (GPX); thyroid deiodinase | Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy) | Selenosis (hair loss, nail brittleness) |
| Iodine (I) | Thyroid hormones (T3, T4) | Hypothyroidism, goiter, cretinism | Thyrotoxicosis |
| Chromium (Cr) | Human essentiality and proposed insulin-related role remain disputed | No clinically defined human deficiency state | Cr(VI) compounds are carcinogenic; this does not establish the same hazard for dietary Cr(III) |
| Fluorine (F), as fluoride | Supports enamel remineralization and caries prevention | Reduced caries protection at low exposure; caries is not proof of a systemic fluoride-deficiency syndrome | Fluorosis (mottled enamel) |

The fluoride distinction concerns prevention rather than a diagnostic deficiency test: U.S. child/adolescent survey data associated community water fluoridation with lower caries experience \citep{slade2018fluoridation}. Magnesium's intestinal laxative effect must likewise be distinguished from systemic toxicity; a fatal cathartic-associated case was reported by Onishi and Yoshino \citep{onishi2006hypermagnesemia}, and the [NIH magnesium fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) identifies impaired renal clearance as a risk factor.

## E.4 Ultra-Trace and Context-Dependent Elements {.unnumbered}

- **Nickel (Ni)** — cofactor in urease (bacterial, plant) and hydrogenase; suspected but not proven essential in mammals.
- **Silicon (Si)** — structural in diatoms, horsetails, and grasses through silica deposition; this does not establish an essential human dietary requirement.
- **Boron (B)** — essential in plants (cell-wall cross-linking via borate–rhamnogalacturonan); possibly beneficial in vertebrates.
- **Vanadium (V)** — accumulated by vanadocytes in some ascidians; accumulation should not be equated with a demonstrated oxygen-transport function.
- **Tungsten (W)** — used instead of molybdenum by hyperthermophilic archaea.

## E.5 Life Without an Expected Element {.unnumbered}

Biology has bent the rules. Examples of organisms that substitute or replace commonly "essential" elements:

- **Claims of iron-independent growth** require a named organism, defined growth medium, and evidence excluding trace iron. An unspecified “iron-free vent bacterium” is not sufficient evidence that manganese can replace every iron-dependent function.
- **Selenocysteine** (the "21st amino acid") — expands the amino-acid alphabet using the rare element Se.
- **Pyrrolysine** (the "22nd amino acid") — used by specialized translation systems in some archaea and bacteria; “22nd” names its place in the expanded genetic code, not its atom count.
- **Arsenic-based life controversy (GFAJ-1, 2010)** — claim of As replacing P in DNA of a *Halomonas* strain; later retracted.

The periodic table remains under-explored for biological innovations. Astrobiological life may use different element sets entirely.
