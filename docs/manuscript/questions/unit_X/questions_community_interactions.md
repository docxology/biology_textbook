1. Distinguish a population, a community, and an ecosystem.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** A population comprises one species in a specified place/time; a community comprises interacting populations of different species. An ecosystem additionally includes abiotic surroundings and their fluxes; the boundary must be stated.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: one-versus-multiple species; 2 points: abiotic boundary. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

2. Give the fitness-effect signs for competition, predation, mutualism, commensalism, parasitism, and amensalism.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Competition −/−; predation +/−; mutualism +/+; commensalism +/0; parasitism +/−; amensalism −/0. Signs describe effects relative to a defined no-interaction comparison, not moral value.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: six correct signs; 2 points: counterfactual definition. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

3. Contrast fundamental and realized niche.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** The fundamental niche is the set of abiotic conditions/resources permitting persistence without the focal limiting biotic interactions. The realized niche reflects actual biotic and dispersal context; facilitation can extend realized occupancy beyond an isolated-species prediction.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: abiotic potential versus realized context; 2 points: facilitation qualification. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

4. Define keystone species without equating it with the most abundant species.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** A keystone has a disproportionately large community effect relative to its abundance/biomass. A removal experiment can reveal it; abundant foundation species can matter strongly by a different abundance-dependent mechanism.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: disproportionate effect; 2 points: experimental versus abundance criterion. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

5. Distinguish primary from secondary succession and identify one retained legacy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** Primary succession begins on substrate without developed soil; secondary succession retains soil and often propagules or roots after disturbance. These legacies affect trajectories, so neither guarantees a fixed sequence ending in the same forest.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: soil/legacy contrast; 2 points: nonuniversal trajectory. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Define richness, Shannon diversity, and evenness.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** Richness is species count $S$; $H=-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i$ combines abundance proportions; $J=H/\ln S$ for $S>1$. Equal richness need not imply equal $H$, and zero observations require a consistent absence/detection rule.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: three definitions; 2 points: richness-evenness distinction. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

7. What would distinguish a stable alternative state from a slow transient?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** A stable state resists small perturbations and returns under the same external conditions. A long time series alone can be a slow transient; perturbation/recovery and changed initial-condition evidence are needed.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: return under fixed forcing; 2 points: transient alternative. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

8. Define connectance for a directed web when self-links are allowed.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** $C=L/S^2$ for $S$ taxa and $L$ directed links when all ordered pairs, including self-links, are possible. Excluding self-links changes the denominator to $S(S-1)$; state the convention before comparing webs.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: L/S²; 2 points: denominator convention. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

9. What assumption makes a neutral community model a useful null model?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** Species have equivalent per-capita demographic performance and composition changes through stochastic birth, death, dispersal, and speciation. A fit to an abundance pattern does not prove equivalence or absence of niche mechanisms.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: equivalence and stochastic processes; 2 points: pattern not unique process. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

10. Distinguish classical, augmentative, and conservation biological control.

<!-- SOLUTION

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: three strategies; 2 points: different risk profiles. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
11. For K1=1000,K2=800,α12=.5,α21=1.2, calculate rare-species invasion margins and predict outcome.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** $u=1000-0.5\times800=600$; $v=800-1.2\times1000=-400$. Species 1 invades species 2, but species 2 cannot invade species 1, so species 1 excludes species 2; division-based reversed criteria are wrong.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: invasion margins of 600 and −400; 2 points: exclusion by species 1 from invasion signs.
SOLUTION -->

16. Compute $H$ and $J$ for counts of 25, 25, 25 and 25; compare with a single-species count of 100.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** The even four-species community has $H=\ln4\approx1.386$ and $J=1$. The single-species community has $H=0$; $J=H/\ln1$ is undefined, so report single-species richness and $H$ rather than divide by zero.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: H/J for even sample; 2 points: single-species domain. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q22, Synthesis).** Randomized replicated removals and sham/unmanipulated controls compare community change before/after intervention \citep{paine1966}. Normalize effect to removed biomass and measure habitat covariates; co-occurrence with diversity alone does not establish a keystone.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: causal removal/control contrast; 2 points: effect per biomass and habitat alternative. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
