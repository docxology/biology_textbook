<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. Define an ecosystem. What are its biotic and abiotic components?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with the physical and chemical environment they interact with, linked by energy flow and nutrient cycling. Biotic components are the living parts: producers (autotrophs), consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores), and decomposers. Abiotic components are nonliving factors: sunlight, temperature, water, air (CO2, O2), inorganic nutrients (N, P), and soil. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: biotic/abiotic boundary; 2 points: energy and matter linkage. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. What is primary productivity? Distinguish GPP, NPP, and NEP.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Primary productivity is the rate at which autotrophs form organic matter; in carbon units, GPP is gross fixation and NPP = GPP − R_a subtracts autotrophic respiration. NEP = NPP − R_h also subtracts heterotrophic respiration. Positive NEP means fixation exceeds these respiratory losses, not necessarily net storage across a boundary with harvest, fire or other exports. Under the chapter's budget, NBP = NEP − D determines net storage: NEP = 100 and D = 150 g C m−2 yr−1 gives NBP = −50. Rates have units such as g C m−2 yr−1, whereas stocks have g C m−2. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: GPP−R_a−R_h hierarchy and carbon-rate units; 2 points: NEP metabolic boundary versus NBP/net storage, with stock-versus-rate distinction. Do not require a sink conclusion from NEP alone.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. What is a trophic level? Name four trophic levels in a food chain.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** A trophic level is a feeding position defined by the number of energy-transfer steps from the energy source. Four levels: producers (autotrophs, grass), primary consumers (herbivores, grasshopper), secondary consumers (carnivores, shrew), tertiary consumers (top carnivores, hawk). Decomposers process dead matter from every level. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: feeding-position definition; 2 points: four correctly ordered trophic levels. The detrital explanation is enrichment, not an additional requirement for credit.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. What is ecological efficiency? What is the typical transfer efficiency between trophic levels?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** Ecological (trophic transfer) efficiency is the percentage of energy at one trophic level incorporated into biomass at the next. The typical value is about 10 percent (Lindeman's ten-percent rule); the rest is lost to respiration, egestion, or goes unconsumed. Because efficiency compounds across levels, food chains are short and top-predator biomass is small. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: production-to-production ratio; 2 points: variable efficiency, not a thermodynamic constant.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. Define the carbon cycle. Name the major carbon reservoirs on Earth.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** The carbon cycle is the global movement of carbon among reservoirs via photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, and ocean exchange. Major reservoirs: the atmosphere (CO2, CH4), the oceans (largest active reservoir, dissolved inorganic carbon), terrestrial biomass and soils, and the very large slow-turnover sedimentary rocks and fossil fuels. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: reservoirs versus flows; 2 points: fast/slow carbon context. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Define the nitrogen cycle. Name four major microbial transformations and state the starting and resulting nitrogen forms for each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** The nitrogen cycle is the redistribution and chemical transformation of nitrogen among biotic and abiotic reservoirs. Four major microbial transformations are biological nitrogen fixation (N2 to ammonia/ammonium by diazotrophs), ammonification (organic N to ammonia/ammonium), nitrification (ammonium to nitrite to nitrate), and denitrification (nitrate reduction toward gaseous N products under low oxygen). Industrial Haber–Bosch fixation also converts N2 to ammonia but is not microbial or nitrogenase-mediated fixation. Keep new fixation distinct from transfers of nitrogen already fixed. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 1 point: cycle definition; 1 point: four microbial transformation names; 2 points: starting/resulting forms and directions for those transformations.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is the phosphorus cycle? How does it differ from the nitrogen and carbon cycles?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** The phosphorus cycle moves P from rock weathering into soil and water as phosphate, through organisms and back to sediments. Unlike carbon and nitrogen, phosphorus lacks a comparably large gaseous reservoir, but phosphorus-bearing dust and aerosols can travel through the atmosphere and be deposited on ecosystems. Yu et al. (2015)'s Saharan-dust study provides a specific example of estimated phosphorus delivery to the Amazon, not a gaseous reservoir. Local P limitation depends on bioavailable supply and demand; it cannot be deduced from gas-phase absence alone. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

The atmospheric-particle example is drawn from Yu et al. \citep{yu2015africandust}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: rock, organism and sediment pathways; 2 points: absence of a large gaseous reservoir distinguished from atmospheric particle transport and local limitation.
SOLUTION -->

8. Write the Martin flux-attenuation law and calculate $F(1000\ \mathrm{m})/F(100\ \mathrm{m})$ for exponent $b=1$.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Application).** The law is $F(z)=F(z_0)(z/z_0)^{-b}$, with positive reference flux at $z_0=100\ \mathrm{m}$. For $b=1$, the requested ratio is $(1000/100)^{-1}=0.1$, or 10% of the reference flux. This power law is not an exponential with a half-depth and does not determine carbon residence time or atmospheric sequestration duration. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: power law with the reference depth specified; 2 points: correct normalized depth-flux ratio, 0.1 (10%).
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is eutrophication? What nutrients cause it and why?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** Eutrophication is nutrient enrichment that can increase production; decomposition of the resulting organic matter can increase oxygen demand and cause hypoxia when supply and ventilation are insufficient. Nitrogen and phosphorus inputs from runoff, sewage and other sources can contribute. Local limitation depends on bioavailability, demand and conditions; a freshwater/marine label or an N:P ratio alone is not an experimental diagnosis \citep{elser2007nutrientlimitation}. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: nutrient enrichment/decomposition; 2 points: oxygen-loss causal chain. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. Define decomposition. What organisms are primary decomposers, and how do termites and ants change the detrital pathway?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Decomposition is the microbial and detritivore breakdown of dead organic matter, releasing inorganic nutrients (mineralization) and CO2 via heterotrophic respiration. Primary decomposers are bacteria and fungi (saprotrophs), assisted by detritivores (earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, soil arthropods) that fragment litter and increase surface area for microbial attack. Termites add symbiotic lignocellulose digestion and mound/galleried soil structure; ants fragment and move organic matter, excavate mineral soil, and redistribute nutrients. They are not primary microbial decomposers, but they engineer the physical and biological conditions under which decomposition proceeds. See \cref{sec:unit_X_ecosystem_ecology}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: microbial/detritivore roles; 2 points: termite-ant engineering distinction. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

12. In an ocean sample with lower phytoplankton standing biomass than zooplankton biomass but higher phytoplankton production, explain the role of turnover. Why need this inversion not hold at every site or instant?

<!-- SOLUTION

Phytoplankton can accumulate during blooms \citep{behrenfeld2010blooms}; inversion is not universal across sites or times, nor do all taxa share a daily division time.
