# Questions — Mendelian Principles and Probability {#sec:q_unit_V_mendelian_principles .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Compute a genetic probability or chi-square result and explain the biological assumption.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Punnett, binomial, and chi-square calculations.
- **Assessment alignment:** Statistical Tests and Data Analysis, Representing and Describing Data.
- **Misconception probe:** Dominant does not mean common, stronger, or better.
- **Transfer product:** Transfer Mendelian probability to model organisms and introductory human genetics.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

## Questions 1–10 {.unnumbered}

*This activity accompanies \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles} of the textbook — review that chapter before attempting the exercises below.*

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. State Mendel's two laws. Give the biological basis for each in terms of meiosis.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** **Law of segregation:** the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation so each gamete carries only one allele; mechanistically this is the separation of homologous chromosomes (and their alleles) at meiosis I anaphase. **Law of independent assortment:** alleles of different genes segregate independently, because non-homologous chromosome pairs orient randomly at the metaphase I plate. Independent assortment holds only for genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on the same one). See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO9; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. How can an autosomal-recessive enzyme defect and a dominant haploinsufficient allele produce different disease patterns while both obey chromosome segregation?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** A heterozygote transmits each allele with probability one half in either case. A recessive enzyme defect is masked when one functional copy supplies enough activity; two defective copies cause disease. In haploinsufficiency one functional copy is insufficient, so heterozygotes can be affected. Dominance describes dosage-to-phenotype response, not preferential allele transmission. Credit both mechanisms and the shared segregation rule. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
3. What is a Punnett square? Draw and solve: Aa × Aa.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Application).** A Punnett square is a table that lists the possible gametes from each parent and combines them to predict offspring genotypes under Mendelian segregation. For *Aa × Aa*, each parent produces gametes *A* and *a*. The four equally likely cells are *AA*, *Aa*, *Aa*, and *aa*, giving a genotype ratio of **1 *AA* : 2 *Aa* : 1 *aa***. With complete dominance, the phenotype ratio is **3 dominant : 1 recessive**. This prediction assumes independent gamete formation, equal fertilization probability, and no viability difference among the genotypes; see \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Distinguish penetrance from expressivity and define a phenocopy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** Penetrance is the fraction of people with a specified genotype who meet the phenotype definition at a specified age/environment. Expressivity is the degree or range of expression among affected people. A phenocopy resembles a genetic phenotype but has a different, often environmental, cause. Neither incomplete penetrance nor a phenocopy implies failed allele segregation. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
5. What are the expected phenotype ratios for a dihybrid cross between two double heterozygotes?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Application).** A dihybrid cross between two double heterozygotes (*AaBb × AaBb*) yields the classic **9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio** — 9 A\_B\_ : 3 A\_bb : 3 aaB\_ : 1 aabb. This is the product of two independent 3:1 monohybrid ratios and requires the two genes to assort independently and act independently on the phenotype. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Define codominance. Give the human blood type system as an example.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** **Codominance** is an allelic relationship in which both alleles in a heterozygote are fully and simultaneously expressed, so neither masks the other. The human ABO system illustrates this: an *I^A I^B* individual is blood type AB, expressing both the A and B surface antigens, rather than an intermediate or single antigen. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
7. What is incomplete dominance? Give one example and show the F₁ and F₂ ratios.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Application).** **Incomplete dominance** occurs when the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype because one functional allele produces insufficient product for the full dominant effect. In snapdragons, red (*C^R C^R*) × white (*C^W C^W*) gives an all-pink F₁ (*C^R C^W*); selfing the F₁ gives an F₂ of **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white** — the genotype ratio is visible directly because each genotype has its own phenotype. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. What is a testcross? For what is it used?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** A testcross mates a dominant-phenotype individual to a homozygous recessive partner. A recessive offspring rules out AA under the stipulated fully penetrant model. All-dominant offspring support but do not prove AA: an Aa parent produces n dominant offspring with probability $(1/2)^n$. Sample size is therefore essential. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is epistasis? Give one example where two loci interact to modify phenotype.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** **Epistasis** is a gene interaction in which the genotype at one locus masks or modifies the phenotypic expression of a second, independent locus. In Labrador retrievers, *ee* at the extension locus is recessive epistatic to the B (black/chocolate) locus: any *ee* dog is yellow regardless of its B genotype, collapsing the dihybrid F₂ to **9 black : 3 chocolate : 4 yellow**. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. What is pleiotropy? Give one example of a single gene that affects multiple traits.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** **Pleiotropy** is the phenomenon in which a single gene affects multiple, seemingly unrelated phenotypic traits. Sickle-cell disease (a single β-globin mutation) is a classic example: it causes anemia, pain crises, organ damage, splenic dysfunction, and stroke risk, while also conferring malaria resistance in heterozygotes. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->



## Questions 11–20 {.unnumbered}

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
11. A black cat (BB) is crossed with a white cat (bb). What is the F₁ phenotype? What is the F₂ ratio if F₁ cats are interbred? If black is incompletely dominant, what is the F₁ phenotype?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** With complete dominance, *BB* × *bb* gives an all-black F₁ (*Bb*). Interbreeding the F₁ (*Bb × Bb*) gives an **F₂ of 3 black : 1 white**. If black is instead *incompletely* dominant, the F₁ heterozygotes (*Bb*) show an **intermediate (gray) phenotype**, and the F₂ would be 1 black : 2 gray : 1 white. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
12. A synthetic Aa×Aa cross yields 450 round and 130 wrinkled seeds. Compute chi-square against 3:1, using critical 3.84 at α=.05 and df=1. What does non-rejection establish?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Expected counts are 435 round and 145 wrinkled. $\chi^2=225/435+225/145=60/29\approx2.069<3.84$, so do not reject the fixed 3:1 model at α=0.05. These data are compatible with that model; the test does not prove the model or identify chance as the actual cause of the deviation. Credit expected counts, both contributions, df=1 and this bounded interpretation. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
13. Two true-breeding strains of wheat produce red and white kernels. The cross produces medium-red F₁, and F₂ shows a continuous range of colors with frequencies approximately 1:4:6:4:1. This is consistent with two genes each contributing an additive unit of color. What mode of inheritance does this illustrate?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q13, Application).** This is **polygenic (quantitative) inheritance** with additive alleles. Two genes each contributing one additive unit of pigment produce 5 phenotypic classes in F₂ with frequencies 1:4:6:4:1 (the binomial expansion of dose 0–4 across 4 alleles), with the medium-red F₁ intermediate between the parental extremes. Environmental variation further blurs the classes into a continuous distribution. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO7; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
14. A woman is phenotypically normal but her father had phenylketonuria (PKU, autosomal recessive). What is the probability that she is a carrier? If she marries a known carrier, what is the probability their first child will have PKU?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q14, Application).** Her father is *aa*, so the woman necessarily inherited one *a* allele; being unaffected she is *Aa* — she is a **carrier with probability 1** (= 100%). Crossed with a known carrier (*Aa × Aa*), each child has a **¼ probability of PKU** (*aa*). Combined chance the first child is affected = 1 × ¼ = **1/4**. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
15. In coat color genetics of Labrador retrievers: B is dominant for black pigment; b is recessive (brown/chocolate). E is dominant for coat expression; ee gives yellow regardless of B/b. Set up the dihybrid cross BbEe × BbEe and calculate the expected ratio of black:chocolate:yellow.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** *BbEe × BbEe* gives the standard 9 B\_E\_ : 3 B\_ee : 3 bbE\_ : 1 bbee. Because *ee* is epistatic (yellow regardless of B), the two *ee* classes merge: 9 B\_E\_ = black, 3 bbE\_ = chocolate, and 3 B\_ee + 1 bbee = 4 yellow. Expected ratio = **9 black : 3 chocolate : 4 yellow**. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
16. Evaluate the concept of "genetic heterogeneity": the same phenotype (e.g., congenital deafness) can result from mutations in any of >100 different genes, each segregating in different families. How does genetic heterogeneity complicate: (a) pedigree analysis; (b) population frequency estimates; (c) the development of a comprehensive gene therapy?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Synthesis).** Locus heterogeneity means a similar phenotype can arise through different loci and inheritance modes, so family-specific genotyping and phenotyping are needed. Population estimates require per-locus risks, penetrance and ascertainment, with attention to overlapping etiologies; simply adding risks can double-count individuals. Gene/allele-specific correction may require different constructs, but interventions at shared downstream pathways could benefit multiple genotypes. Heterogeneity alone proves neither a unique frequency formula nor impossibility of a shared treatment. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Create; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
17. Design a genetic screen in *C. elegans* to identify most genes involved in a specific pathway (e.g., vulva development). Describe the EMS (ethyl methanesulfonate) mutagenesis strategy, the phenotypic screen, and how you would determine whether two mutations affect the same gene (complementation test) or the same pathway (epistasis test).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Synthesis).** Screen descendants of independent EMS-treated parents for a prespecified vulval phenotype, with untreated isogenic controls, blinded scoring and sufficient progeny to recover recessive mutations. Cross recessive mutants for complementation, validate with independent alleles and rescue, then compare each single mutant and double mutant. Pathway ordering requires the specified activating/inhibitory relationships and allele functions; the epistatic phenotype is not universally the downstream gene. Credit a practical screen, controls and a discriminating model. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
18. Quantitative traits (height, intelligence, blood pressure) are determined by many genes plus environment. Evaluate the methodology of heritability estimation from twin studies: what does h² = 0.8 actually mean, and why does high heritability NOT mean that environmental interventions are ineffective? Use PKU as a concrete counter-example.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q18, Synthesis).** Narrow-sense h²=VA/VP, so h²=.8 assigns 80% of phenotypic variance to additive genetic variance in the stated population/environment model. Broad-sense H² includes nonadditive genetic variance. Twin estimates depend on assumptions about shared environments, mating and genetic/environmental contributions; a twin correlation alone does not identify every variance component. Neither ratio partitions an individual's trait or rules out environmental intervention. Early appropriate PKU management illustrates modifiability without guaranteeing every clinical outcome. Award definitions, design assumptions and the variance-versus-intervention distinction. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
19. Evaluate Mendel's choice of pea traits: several of the seven traits happened to be either not linked or on different chromosomes. If two of Mendel's traits had been tightly linked (say, flower color and pod color on the same chromosome), how would the F₂ data have deviated from the 9:3:3:1 ratio, and would Mendel still have discovered independent assortment?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** If two traits were tightly linked, their alleles would not assort independently: parental allele combinations would be over-represented and recombinant types rare, so the F₂ would deviate sharply from **9:3:3:1** toward an excess of parental phenotypes (the deviation scaling with map distance / recombination frequency). Mendel could still have discovered segregation and independent assortment from his *other* unlinked traits, but the linked pair alone would have masked independent assortment — historically fortunate that his seven analyzed traits behaved independently. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

## Questions 21–30 {.unnumbered}

<!-- assess: LO=LO9; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
21. Anticipation (increasing severity across generations) occurs in trinucleotide repeat disorders (Huntington's disease, myotonic dystrophy). Explain the molecular mechanism by which repeats expand between generations (slippage during DNA replication) and why larger repeats cause earlier onset and greater severity. Why is repeat expansion more common through the paternal germline in HD but the maternal germline in DM1?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q21, Application).** Trinucleotide repeats (e.g. CAG in *HTT*, CTG in *DMPK*) expand through **replication/repair slippage**: the nascent strand mispairs on the repetitive template, forming hairpin loops that add repeat units, and longer tracts are more unstable, so they tend to grow each generation (**anticipation**). Larger repeats produce more toxic gene product (or RNA) earlier, giving **earlier onset and greater severity**. Expansion bias is **germline-specific**: large CAG expansions are favored in spermatogenesis (paternal bias in Huntington's), whereas the CTG repeat expands preferentially in the maternal germline in myotonic dystrophy type 1, reflecting differences in replication/repair dynamics in the two gametogenic lineages. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
22. Evaluate the concept of "genomic imprinting conflict" (kinship theory): paternally expressed genes (the maternal allele is silenced, or maternally imprinted) are generally growth-promoting (*IGF2*), while maternally expressed genes (the paternal allele is silenced, or paternally imprinted) are generally growth-restraining (*H19*). Evaluate the evolutionary logic that predicts this based on parent-offspring conflict theory. What happens when this balance is disrupted (Silver-Russell syndrome vs Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q22, Synthesis).** Kinship theory makes conditional predictions from parent-of-origin relatedness asymmetries: an allele can favor different maternal-resource allocation depending on its expected relatedness to other offspring. Multiple paternity is a common illustration, not the only possible source of asymmetry. Growth effects must be checked by locus, tissue and stage; the IGF2/H19 example is not a rule for every imprinted gene. Reduced or increased IGF2 activity contributes to some Silver–Russell or Beckwith–Wiedemann cases, respectively, but their heterogeneous molecular etiologies are not exhausted by one IGF2 mechanism. Award the conditional logic and distinguish evolutionary hypothesis from syndrome diagnosis. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
23. Multifactorial diseases (Type 1 Diabetes, schizophrenia) have both genetic and environmental contributions. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of GWAS and polygenic risk scores: (a) minimum detectable effect size at genome-wide significance (p < 5×10⁻⁸); (b) the "missing heritability" problem; (c) why PRS transferability across ancestries is a scientific and equity concern; (d) what consent and privacy information belongs in a clinical PRS report.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q23, Synthesis).** (a) GWAS detects variants whose effect is large enough that, given allele frequency and sample size, association reaches p < 5×10⁻⁸; common variants of small effect need very large cohorts and rare large-effect variants are missed. (b) The **missing heritability** problem: significant common variants typically explain a modest fraction of trait heritability, far below twin-study estimates, due to many sub-threshold variants, rare variants, gene–gene/gene–environment effects, and inflated twin estimates. (c) A PRS trained mainly in one ancestry can lose calibration and discrimination in another because linkage disequilibrium, allele frequency, environment, and health-system context differ; deployment without validation can therefore worsen health disparities. (d) A clinical report should state the discovery cohort, target population, calibration method, confidence interval, action threshold, data-retention plan, secondary-use policy, reidentification risk, and family implications. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
24. Phenocopies are environmentally caused conditions that resemble genetic conditions. Thalidomide-induced limb malformations in 1950s–60s phenocopied the autosomal recessive condition phocomelia. Evaluate: (a) how phenocopies complicate pedigree analysis; (b) how you would distinguish a phenocopy from a genetic condition in a clinical or research context; (c) the ethical implications of confusing genetic and environmental causation for public health policy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q24, Synthesis).** A phenocopy is an environmentally induced phenotype mimicking a genetic disorder. (a) It complicates pedigrees by appearing as sporadic or non-Mendelian cases (no consistent transmission, possible shared environmental exposure rather than shared genotype). (b) Distinguish it by molecular testing (absence of the causative mutation), a documented environmental exposure (e.g. thalidomide timing), discordance in monozygotic twins, and lack of recurrence risk consistent with Mendelian ratios. (c) Misattributing an environmental cause to genetics — or vice versa — misdirects public-health policy: it can stigmatize families, divert resources from removing a preventable exposure, and delay protective regulation. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
25. Critically evaluate Mendel's pea experiments, considering: (a) sample sizes (he used thousands of plants, giving high statistical power); (b) evidence that Mendel may have "improved" his data (Weldon's 1902 re-analysis and Ronald Fisher's 1936 statistical critique suggesting perfect ratios are too good to be true); (c) whether Mendel's results are reproducible, and what this debate reveals about the role of confirmation bias in science.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q25, Synthesis).** (a) Mendel's large samples (thousands of plants) gave high statistical power, making his ratios reliable estimates. (b) Fisher's 1936 analysis (anticipated by Weldon, 1902) showed the aggregate fit to expected ratios is *too good* — the pooled χ² is improbably small — suggesting unconscious bias in classification or selective reporting, though no fraud is established. (c) Mendel's core conclusions are robustly **reproducible** in modern crosses, so the laws stand; the debate illustrates that even correct conclusions can be reached via data that show confirmation bias, underscoring the need for pre-specified analysis and blinded scoring rather than discarding the findings. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

26. ABO blood type shows codominance (A and B antigens) and simple recessive (O, no antigen). A man of unknown blood type has a type O child with a type A woman (confirmed genotype IA^i). What is the man's blood type? What are most possible blood types of this union's children?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q26, Application).** The type O child is *ii*, so it received an *i* from each parent. The type A mother is *I^A i*, contributing *i*; the father must therefore also carry an *i* allele and be capable of producing a type O child — his blood type can be **A (*I^A i*), B (*I^B i*), or O (*ii*)**, but not AB. For an *I^A i × i i* (e.g. type O father) union, children are **½ type A (*I^A i*) : ½ type O (*ii*)**; the full set across his possible genotypes is type A, B, AB, or O depending on his second allele. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
27. Maternal effect genes in *Drosophila* (e.g., *bicoid*) determine the embryo's polarity based on the maternal genotype, not the embryo's genotype. Explain how this violates the simple Mendelian expectation that offspring phenotype reflects offspring genotype. What does this tell us about developmental genetics?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q27, Recall).** With maternal-effect genes such as *bicoid*, the **mother's genotype**, acting through mRNA/protein she deposits in the oocyte, fixes the embryo's phenotype regardless of the embryo's own genotype — so a genotypically mutant embryo from a heterozygous mother develops normally, and phenotypes lag one generation behind genotypes. This violates the Mendelian expectation that an offspring's phenotype reflects its own genotype, and shows that early development is governed by maternally supplied cytoplasmic determinants, not just zygotic transcription. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
28. For an explicit fully recessive hemophilia A phenotype model, cross X^H X^h with X^H Y, assume independent births and equal sex probabilities, and give daughter/son genotype probabilities. Compute the probability of at least two affected sons among four children. Why must the modeled daughter phenotype not be used as a guarantee for real heterozygous women?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q28, Application).** Each sex–genotype class has probability 1/4: X^H X^H, X^H X^h, X^H Y and X^h Y. Conditional on daughters, half are noncarriers and half heterozygotes; conditional on sons, half are affected in the stipulated model. P(affected son per birth)=1/4, so P(K≥2)=1−(3/4)^4−4(1/4)(3/4)^3=67/256≈.2617. The fully recessive model treats heterozygous daughters as unaffected, but real heterozygous women can have bleeding symptoms, including clinically significant hemophilia \citep{cdc2024hemophiliawomen}; carrier genotype is not a phenotype guarantee. Award conditional probabilities, binomial calculation and the clinical boundary. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
29. For independently assorting loci in AaBb × AaBb, pigment appears only in A_B_; A_bb, aaB_ and aabb are unpigmented. Derive the expected phenotype ratio. What functional requirement follows, and can the ratio distinguish two sequential enzymes from two required transcriptional regulators? Propose discriminating evidence.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q29, Application).** Independent segregation gives 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb, hence 9 pigmented : 7 unpigmented. Under the supplied phenotype rule, a dominant function at each locus is required. Two non-redundant sequential enzymes are compatible, but so are two regulators jointly required to activate a pigment gene; both yield (3/4)(3/4) = 9/16 pigmented offspring. The ratio alone identifies neither enzyme function nor pathway order. Molecular characterization, intermediate supplementation and appropriate gene-function perturbations can distinguish these architectures. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: 9:7 from independent segregation and both required functions; 2 points: an alternative architecture plus discriminating molecular or supplementation evidence. Do not award a unique linear-pathway conclusion from counts alone.
SOLUTION -->

30. In explicit synthetic R? × rr testcrosses, A yields 8 round/0 wrinkled; B yields 2 round/2 wrinkled; C yields 3 round/1 wrinkled. Which parental genotypes are compatible? Compute the probability of C's result if C is Rr; can A be proved RR?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** B and C must be Rr under complete penetrance and correct scoring because each produces rr offspring. A may be RR or Rr: the probability of eight round under Rr is $(1/2)^8=1/256$. For C, $P(3\text{ round of }4)=\binom43(1/2)^4=1/4$. A 3:1 sample is not the expected ratio, but is quite possible under a 1:1 testcross model; no contamination or extra parent is required. See \cref{sec:unit_V_mendelian_principles}.
SOLUTION -->
