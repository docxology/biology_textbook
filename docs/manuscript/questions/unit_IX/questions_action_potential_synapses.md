<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. Define an action potential and give illustrative neuronal threshold, peak, and undershoot voltages. Are these universal, and can a spatially uniform four-ODE model describe propagation by itself?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** An action potential is a regenerative membrane-voltage excursion. In an illustrative sodium-spiking neuron, rest may be about −70 mV, threshold −55 mV, peak +30 to +40 mV, and undershoot −80 to −90 mV, with a duration around 1–2 ms. These are not universal neuronal landmarks: conductances, preparation and stimulation history matter. Rest need not equal E_K because multiple permeabilities and electrogenic transport contribute. The local Hodgkin–Huxley equations evolve V, m, h and n for a specified membrane preparation; propagation additionally requires spatial/cable coupling and axonal parameters. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: AP definition and representative voltages; 2 points: threshold depends on model and cell.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. What are voltage-gated Na⁺ channels? Describe their three states (closed, open, inactivated).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Voltage-gated sodium channels activate on depolarization and can carry the regenerative inward current of a sodium spike. Closed, available channels can open; open channels conduct; inactivated channels have reduced availability until recovery, usually favored by repolarization. The m and h variables summarize activation and inactivation kinetics phenomenologically, not literal universal gates or fixed timings. Sodium-channel inactivation contributes to refractoriness and normal forward propagation but does not determine propagation direction independently of stimulation geometry. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: closed, open and inactivated states; 2 points: activation versus recovery.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. What is the absolute refractory period? What is the relative refractory period?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** The absolute refractory interval is operationally the interval after a spike during which the specified membrane and stimulation protocol cannot evoke another spike, largely because sodium-channel availability is reduced. During relative refractoriness, recovery and residual outward conductance permit another spike with a stronger stimulus. Illustrative intervals of 1–2 ms and 5–10 ms are not fixed across neurons. Local refractoriness contributes to normal forward propagation but does not prevent bidirectional propagation when an axon is stimulated in its middle; it does not set a universal firing-rate ceiling. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: Na inactivation/afterconductance; 2 points: absolute versus relative excitability. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. What is a compound action potential (CAP)? How does it differ from a single-neuron AP?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** A single-neuron action potential is the all-or-none ~100 mV spike of one axon, described by the Hodgkin–Huxley equations from the squid giant axon. A **compound action potential (CAP)** is the extracellularly recorded summed electrical signal of a whole nerve containing many axons with different diameters and conduction velocities. It differs from a single AP in being a graded, multi-peaked waveform: its amplitude grows with stimulus strength (recruiting more fibers, not "bigger" spikes), and it spreads out into separate humps with distance because fast (large, myelinated A) and slow (small, unmyelinated C) fibers progressively desynchronise. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: population CAP summation; 2 points: fiber latency/size distinction. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. Define a synapse. Name the pre- and postsynaptic components.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** A synapse is the specialized junction at which one neuron communicates with a target cell. At a chemical synapse the **presynaptic** component is the axon terminal, containing neurotransmitter-filled vesicles, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, and the active-zone release machinery (SNARE proteins, synaptotagmin-1). These are separated by the ~20 nm **synaptic cleft** from the **postsynaptic** component — the membrane bearing neurotransmitter receptors (ionotropic and metabotropic) clustered at the postsynaptic density. Transmission is unidirectional (presynaptic → postsynaptic), modifiable, and has a 0.5–2 ms synaptic delay; electrical synapses (gap junctions) instead couple cytoplasms directly and are bidirectional. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: pre/post components; 2 points: vesicle-to-receptor transfer. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. What is a neurotransmitter vesicle? How is it released (Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** A synaptic vesicle stores a packet of transmitter. An arriving action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; local Ca²⁺ binds a fast sensor such as synaptotagmin-1, which couples to membranes and the SNARE fusion machinery (synaptobrevin, syntaxin and SNAP-25), releasing transmitter into the cleft. A steep calcium–release slope describes functional sensitivity, not by itself the number of ions or sensor molecules required for fusion. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: Ca/synaptotagmin/SNARE; 2 points: vesicle fusion endpoint. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What are excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs)? Explain their effects on firing using synaptic reversal potential, conductance and ionic driving force. Must inhibition hyperpolarize the cell?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** Excitatory input increases the probability of firing under the specified conditions; inhibitory input reduces it. Opening a synaptic conductance drives membrane voltage toward that conductance's reversal potential, with current determined by conductance and driving force, $I=g(V_m-E_{\mathrm{rev}})$. Glutamate-gated cation conductances commonly produce depolarizing EPSPs. GABA_A and glycine receptors conduct anions, whereas GABA_B signaling can activate potassium conductance. Neither chloride flow direction nor voltage polarity is universal: it depends on ionic gradients and membrane voltage. Increased inhibitory conductance can shunt excitatory current even with little voltage change or with depolarization when its reversal potential and integration effects still limit firing. Kirmse et al.'s P3–4 mouse neocortex preparation provides a specific example of depolarizing GABA with inhibition of network activity, not a rule for every immature neuron \citep{kirmse2015gaba}. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: reversal-potential/conductance logic; 2 points: depolarization not always excitation. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. Define temporal and spatial summation of postsynaptic potentials.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Postsynaptic potentials are graded. **Temporal summation** combines responses from successive inputs arriving before earlier responses have decayed; **spatial summation** combines inputs at different synaptic locations. In a passive approximation, voltage changes can add, but real integration also depends on conductance, reversal potential, location and timing. Inhibition can hyperpolarize or shunt excitatory current rather than simply subtract a fixed voltage. An action potential occurs when the spike-initiation region reaches its condition-dependent threshold; −55 mV is an illustrative value, not a universal boundary. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: temporal/spatial input patterns; 2 points: threshold integration. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is a neuromuscular junction? What neurotransmitter is released there?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** The neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is the chemical synapse between a motor neuron's axon terminal and a skeletal muscle fiber's motor end-plate. The neurotransmitter released there is **acetylcholine (ACh)**: an action potential opens presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels, ACh-containing vesicles fuse and release ACh, which binds muscle-type nicotinic ACh receptors (α1₂β1δε pentamers) opening Na⁺/K⁺ channels to generate the end-plate potential. The NMJ has a high quantal content and resting release probability ~0.1–0.4, giving a large safety factor; ACh is then cleared by acetylcholinesterase. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: ACh and nicotinic NMJ receptors; 2 points: motor-to-muscle architecture.
SOLUTION -->

10. What is long-term potentiation (LTP)? In which brain region was it first described?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Long-term potentiation (LTP) is an activity-dependent, long-lasting (hours to days) increase in synaptic efficacy following high-frequency stimulation — a leading cellular model of learning and memory. It was first described in the **hippocampus**, at the perforant-path inputs to the dentate gyrus (Bliss and Lømo, 1973), and is classically studied at the Schaffer-collateral → CA1 synapse. NMDA-receptor-dependent Ca²⁺ influx activates CaMKII, which phosphorylates and inserts AMPA receptors (early LTP); late LTP additionally requires CREB-driven gene expression and structural spine changes. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: persistent efficacy change; 2 points: hippocampal historical context. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

11. Using the Nernst equation (E = (RT/zF) ln([ion]_out/[ion]_in)), calculate the equilibrium potential for K⁺ given [K⁺]_out = 5 mM and [K⁺]_in = 150 mM at 37°C. Then calculate E_Na with [Na⁺]_out = 145 mM and [Na⁺]_in = 15 mM. Which equilibrium potential is closest to the resting membrane potential?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** At body temperature 310 K, $RT/F = (8.314 \times 310)/96485 = 0.0267$ V = 26.7 mV; using base-10 log, $(RT/F)\ln 10 = 26.7 \times 2.303 = 61.5$ mV. For a monovalent cation ($z = +1$): $E = 61.5 \log_{10}([X]_{out}/[X]_{in})$ mV. **E_K**: $61.5 \times \log_{10}(5/150) = 61.5 \times \log_{10}(0.0333) = 61.5 \times (-1.477) = \mathbf{-90.8\ \mathrm{mV}}$. **E_Na**: $61.5 \times \log_{10}(145/15) = 61.5 \times \log_{10}(9.667) = 61.5 \times 0.985 = \mathbf{+60.6\ \mathrm{mV}}$. Resting membrane potential ≈ **−70 mV** — much closer to **E_K (−91 mV)** than E_Na (+61 mV), indicating that K⁺ permeability dominates at rest (P_K : P_Na : P_Cl ≈ 1 : 0.04 : 0.45). Why not exactly E_K? Because of small but non-zero Na⁺ leak, and the electrogenic Na⁺/K⁺ pump (see Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz). **Clinical significance**: hyperkalaemia (e.g., [K⁺]_out = 8 mM raises E_K to −80 mV) *depolarizes* the resting potential — at rest the membrane is closer to threshold, but sustained depolarization inactivates Nav channels → **muscle weakness, cardiac conduction block, asystole** (EKG peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → flatline). This is why hyperkalaemia is a medical emergency. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: $E_K$ about −90.9 mV and $E_{Na}$ about +60.6 mV; 2 points: dominant resting potassium permeability interpretation.
SOLUTION -->

15. Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease where antibodies target nicotinic ACh receptors at the NMJ. Predict: (a) whether the EPSP amplitude at the NMJ is increased or decreased; (b) the clinical manifestation (fatigable muscle weakness); (c) why acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) temporarily improve symptoms.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** Initiating condition: autoantibodies bind and crosslink/internalise muscle nicotinic ACh receptors at the NMJ, reducing functional receptor number. (a) With fewer receptors, the same quantal ACh release produces a smaller end-plate potential — **EPSP/end-plate-potential amplitude is decreased**, sometimes failing to reach the muscle fiber's threshold so transmission fails at some junctions. (b) Clinically this gives **fatigable muscle weakness**: with repetitive activity presynaptic ACh release normally declines (rundown), and because the safety factor is already eroded, more junctions fail as activity continues — weakness worsens with use and improves with rest (ocular, bulbar, limb muscles). (c) Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) block ACh breakdown in the cleft, so each released quantum acts longer and on more of the remaining receptors, raising the end-plate potential above threshold and temporarily improving strength. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: reduced receptor safety factor; 2 points: AChE inhibitor symptomatic mechanism. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

16. In a stipulated chloride-only model of GABA_A current, an immature neuron has E_Cl = −40 mV and resting potential −55 mV; an adult comparison has E_Cl = −70 mV. Explain (a) the direction of the initial voltage response; (b) how developmental KCC2 upregulation can alter it; and (c) why depolarization alone establishes neither increased firing nor the efficacy of a neonatal seizure treatment. Identify the missing threshold/conductance evidence.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** (a) In the chloride-only model, opening GABA_A channels initially drives the immature cell from −55 mV toward −40 mV: a depolarizing response. 17. Short-term plasticity: paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) occurs when the second of two closely-spaced stimuli evokes a larger EPSP. Explain the residual calcium hypothesis: Ca²⁺ from the first stimulus remains and adds to Ca²⁺ from the second stimulus, producing greater vesicle release. Predict: would PPF be increased or decreased by a Ca²⁺ chelator (BAPTA)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Application).** Initiating condition: paired-pulse facilitation occurs when two stimuli arrive within tens–hundreds of ms and the second EPSP is larger. Changed component: Ca²⁺ entering on the first action potential is not fully cleared (pumped/buffered) before the second spike. Intermediate mechanism: this **residual Ca²⁺** sums with the Ca²⁺ influx from the second action potential; because release probability rises super-linearly (~4th power) with presynaptic Ca²⁺, the elevated total Ca²⁺ raises p and more vesicles fuse. Observable outcome: the second EPSP is larger (facilitation), most prominent at low-p synapses (e.g., parallel-fiber → Purkinje, p ≈ 0.05). Prediction: a fast Ca²⁺ chelator such as **BAPTA** mops up the residual Ca²⁺ before the second spike, so PPF is **decreased/abolished** — directly testing and supporting the residual-calcium hypothesis. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: residual calcium and facilitation; 2 points: chelator and location controls.
SOLUTION -->

19. Synaptic vesicle recycling occurs via three modes: kiss-and-run (transient fusion pore, rapid), full collapse/clathrin-mediated endocytosis (5–20 s), and bulk endocytosis (during intense stimulation). Explain: (a) how each mode is suited to different firing rates; (b) what happens when recycling capacity is exceeded (synaptic depression); (c) the role of dynamin GTPase in clathrin-mediated endocytosis.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** Initiating condition: vesicle fusion adds membrane and depletes the releasable pool, so terminals must retrieve membrane and refill vesicles. (a) **Kiss-and-run** (transient fusion pore, then resealing) is fast and conserves the vesicle, suited to low/moderate firing where rapid local reuse keeps up with demand; **full-collapse / clathrin-mediated endocytosis** (~5–20 s) cleanly recovers vesicle components at moderate sustained rates; **bulk endocytosis** internalises large membrane infoldings during intense, high-frequency activity when single-vesicle retrieval cannot match the membrane added. (b) When firing outpaces recycling, the readily releasable pool is not replenished and the synapse shows **short-term depression** (declining EPSP amplitude) until activity slows and pools refill. (c) **Dynamin** is a GTPase that assembles a collar around the neck of the budding clathrin-coated pit and, on GTP hydrolysis, mediates fission to pinch off the recycled vesicle; without dynamin function endocytosis stalls and vesicles cannot be retrieved. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: recycling-mode tradeoffs; 2 points: dynamin and depletion. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

20. At cerebellar parallel-fiber–Purkinje-cell synapses, paired parallel- and climbing-fiber activation can induce LTD through a large calcium signal, PKC activation and AMPA-receptor internalization. Explain: (a) why its calcium rule must not be equated with a conventional hippocampal NMDA-dependent LTP/LTD example; (b) how an error-related climbing-fiber signal can guide input-specific plasticity; (c) how such plasticity may contribute to vestibulo-ocular reflex adaptation without being its only possible substrate.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q20, Application).** (a) In the named cerebellar preparation, coincident parallel- and climbing-fiber activity can generate a large postsynaptic Ca²⁺ signal that recruits PKC-dependent signaling and AMPA-receptor internalization, weakening parallel-fiber input. Coesmans et al.'s rat experiments found a higher calcium threshold for parallel-fiber LTD than for parallel-fiber LTP. This is the inverse of the conventional calcium ordering often used for hippocampal NMDA-dependent plasticity, where a stronger calcium signal can recruit CaMKII-dependent potentiation and other induction patterns favor depression. The latter is a separately scoped example, not a universal rule imposed on Purkinje cells. (b) An error-related climbing-fiber signal paired with active parallel-fiber inputs can change their later influence on Purkinje-cell firing; the sign and behavioral effect depend on the circuit and protocol. (c) Plasticity in cerebellar pathways can retune vestibulo-ocular reflex gain in response to retinal slip. Parallel-fiber LTD is a candidate contributor within a distributed learning system, not the exclusive established substrate of every form of reflex adaptation or a guarantee of perfect correction. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

These mechanisms have support from separate preparations: Coesmans et al. tested the calcium-ordering contrast in rat parallel-fiber plasticity \citep{coesmans2004cerebellarplasticity}; De Zeeuw et al. found that Purkinje-targeted PKC inhibition blocked LTD in cultured cells and impaired VOR adaptation in transgenic mice \citep{dezeeuw1998pkc}; Wang and Linden interfered with postsynaptic clathrin-mediated endocytosis and blocked LTD in cultured Purkinje neurons \citep{wang2000endocytosis}. No single assay establishes the entire pathway or makes LTD the sole substrate of motor learning.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: named cerebellar high-calcium/PKC/AMPA-receptor-loss mechanism and distinction from the hippocampal example; 2 points: input-specific error learning and non-exclusive contribution to motor adaptation.
SOLUTION -->

23. Evaluate the "synaptic tagging and capture" hypothesis (Frey and Morris, 1997): early LTP is protein synthesis-independent (lasts ~1–2 h), while late LTP requires new protein synthesis. A weak stimulus sets a "tag" at activated synapses; the tag captures plasticity-related proteins (PRPs) synthesized in response to a strong stimulus at another input. Evaluate: (a) the evidence for this model; (b) how it explains input-specificity of LTP; (c) implications for memory consolidation.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q23, Synthesis).** Empirical claim: early LTP is protein-synthesis-independent (~1–2 h) whereas late LTP requires new protein synthesis (CREB-dependent gene expression and structural change). (a) Evidence for synaptic tagging and capture (Frey & Morris, 1997): a weak tetanus that alone gives only decaying early LTP can be converted to persistent late LTP if a strong tetanus is delivered to a *separate* input on the same neuron shortly before/after — the strong input supplies diffusible plasticity-related proteins (PRPs) that are "captured" by a transient local tag set at the weakly stimulated synapse; protein-synthesis inhibitors block this rescue. (b) It explains **input-specificity with shared resources**: the tag is local and short-lived, so only recently activated synapses capture the cell-wide PRP pool, keeping potentiation specific to active inputs while allowing associative interactions across inputs. (c) Implications for **memory consolidation**: behavioral tagging predicts that a weak experience can be consolidated into long-term memory if a salient (PRP-inducing) event occurs nearby in time — a cellular basis for how arousal/novelty stabilizes otherwise transient memories. The judgement is well supported but conditional: it depends on tag identity (still incompletely defined) and on the temporal window — evidence that capture occurs without any local tag would weaken the model. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: weak/strong-input tagging evidence; 2 points: protein-synthesis/input-specific controls. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
