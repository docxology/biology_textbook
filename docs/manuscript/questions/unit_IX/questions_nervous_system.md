1. Name the two divisions of the nervous system. What does each contain?

4. What is an action potential? Describe the four phases (resting, depolarization, repolarization, hyperpolarization).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** An action potential is an all-or-none, regenerative depolarization that propagates along the axon. Phases: resting ($-70$ mV, K$^+$ leak dominant); depolarization (voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels open, $V_m$ rises toward $+30$ mV); repolarization (Na$^+$ channels inactivate, voltage-gated K$^+$ channels open, $V_m$ falls); and hyperpolarization (K$^+$ channels close slowly, dipping below rest before returning to $-70$ mV). See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: regenerative AP phases; 2 points: channel-gating order. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

5. What is the myelin sheath? Name the cells that produce myelin in the CNS and PNS.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** The myelin sheath is a multilayered (up to ~100 wraps) insulating wrap of glial plasma membrane around an axon that increases membrane resistance and speeds conduction. Oligodendrocytes produce CNS myelin (one cell myelinates up to ~50 axons); Schwann cells produce PNS myelin (one cell per axon internode). See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

6. What is saltatory conduction? How does it increase conduction velocity?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** During saltatory propagation along an intact myelinated axon, spikes regenerate at nodes of Ranvier with concentrated voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels. Internodal voltage still changes: current spreads through the axon while myelin raises membrane resistance and reduces effective capacitance, improving conduction for a given diameter. This describes propagation, not a universal site of initial spike generation, and does not make ATP cost proportional to nodal area. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system,sec:unit_IX_action_potential_synapses}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: nodal regeneration; 2 points: resistance/capacitance effects. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

7. Define a synapse. Distinguish chemical from electrical synapses.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** A synapse is the junction where one neuron communicates with a target cell. Chemical synapses transmit via neurotransmitter released from presynaptic vesicles (triggered by Ca$^{2+}$ influx through Cav channels) acting on postsynaptic receptors, giving signal gain and a ~0.5 ms delay; electrical synapses use gap junctions for direct, bidirectional, near-instantaneous ionic current flow. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

12. Multiple sclerosis (MS) destroys myelin in the CNS. Predict: (a) the effect on conduction velocity; (b) which neuronal functions are impaired first; (c) why remyelination therapies are being investigated; (d) why the PNS is relatively spared (different myelinating cells).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** (a) Conduction velocity falls sharply: loss of myelin lowers membrane resistance and raises capacitance, so passive current leaks out and saltatory conduction fails (slowed or blocked). (b) Functions requiring fast, heavily myelinated, long tracts fail first — vision (optic neuritis), motor coordination, sensory and bladder control. (c) Remyelination would restore the insulation needed for saltatory conduction and reverse deficits. (d) The PNS is relatively spared because Schwann cells (not the CNS oligodendrocytes/MOG-MBP autoantigens targeted in MS) make peripheral myelin. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

14. The patellar (knee-jerk) reflex involves a monosynaptic stretch reflex arc. Trace the pathway: muscle spindle → Ia afferent → spinal cord (monosynaptic excitation of α-motor neuron) → quadriceps contraction. Explain: (a) why the reflex is monosynaptic (no interneuron); (b) the role of reciprocal inhibition of the hamstring (via an inhibitory interneuron).

<!-- SOLUTION

15. Sensory adaptation: olfactory receptors rapidly adapt to constant odor exposure (within minutes). Explain: (a) the molecular mechanism (cAMP → Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → reduced CNG channel sensitivity); (b) the difference between phasic (rapidly adapting) and tonic (slowly adapting) receptors; (c) one example of a tonic receptor.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** (a) Sustained odorant raises cAMP, opening CNG channels; Ca$^{2+}$ entering binds calmodulin, which reduces the channel's cAMP sensitivity (negative feedback), so the receptor current declines despite continued stimulus. (b) Phasic (rapidly adapting) receptors respond mainly to stimulus change and fall silent during steady stimulation; tonic (slowly adapting) receptors fire continuously, encoding sustained stimulus intensity. (c) A tonic receptor example is the Merkel disc (sustained pressure) or muscle spindle (sustained stretch/position). See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: calcium/calmodulin feedback; 2 points: phasic/tonic comparison. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

16. Explain the blood-brain barrier's endothelial basis and supporting cells. Why is a 400-Da/lipophilicity rule not an absolute predictor of entry, and how does L-DOPA use carrier-mediated transport?

<!-- SOLUTION

17. Neuroplasticity: after a stroke destroys the motor cortex controlling the right hand, the affected right hand may regain dexterity over weeks. Explain: (a) cortical remapping (adjacent cortical areas take over); (b) the role of long-term potentiation (LTP) in this process; (c) why intensive rehabilitation (constraint-induced movement therapy) accelerates recovery.

<!-- SOLUTION

19. Pain processing: nociceptors detect tissue damage and transmit signals via Aδ (sharp, fast) and C (dull, slow) fibers. Explain the gate control theory: how does activation of large-diameter Aβ touch fibers "close the gate" in the spinal cord dorsal horn, reducing pain transmission to the brain?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** Gate control: pain-carrying Aδ/C fibers and large Aβ touch fibers both project to the dorsal horn. 20. Glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia) have region-dependent abundances and specialized functions. Describe two functional roles of astrocytes beyond structural support: (a) glutamate uptake at synapses (preventing excitotoxicity); (b) regulation of extracellular K⁺; (c) the "tripartite synapse" concept (astrocyte modulates synaptic strength via gliotransmitters).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q20, Application).** (a) Glutamate uptake: astrocyte EAAT1/EAAT2 transporters clear ~90% of synaptic glutamate, terminating signaling and preventing excitotoxic over-activation of NMDA receptors. (c) Tripartite synapse: the astrocyte process is a functional third partner that senses synaptic glutamate (astrocytic Ca$^{2+}$ rise) and releases gliotransmitters (glutamate, ATP, D-serine) that modulate synaptic strength and plasticity. **Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: astrocytic glutamate and potassium roles; 2 points: gliotransmission evidence limits.

21. Evaluate the "integrate-and-fire" model of neuronal computation: a neuron summates thousands of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs and IPSPs) and fires when threshold is reached. What are the limitations of this model (dendritic computation, non-linear integration, backpropagating APs), and how do modern computational neuroscience models address them?

27. Chronic pain can involve maladaptive plasticity (central sensitization) in the spinal cord dorsal horn. Evaluate: (a) how NMDA-dependent activity, glia and BDNF can contribute; (b) how opioid analgesia differs from opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH), which can occur but is not inevitable, and how OIH must be distinguished from tolerance, withdrawal and disease progression; (c) the mechanisms and limits of non-opioid approaches such as gabapentinoids, SNRIs and spinal cord stimulation.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q27, Synthesis).** (a) Repeated nociceptive input can recruit NMDA-dependent activity and longer-lasting changes in dorsal-horn excitability. Glial signaling and BDNF can contribute in studied preparations; short-term wind-up is not identical to persistent central sensitization. (b) Opioids can relieve pain through μ-receptor-mediated inhibition of nociceptive transmission, but OIH is a possible adverse effect in which opioid exposure increases pain sensitivity. It is not an inevitable consequence of chronic use. FDA's April 2023 communication states that OIH can occur at any dose and may occur more often at higher doses or with longer use; reported cases do not establish an individual's probability. Worsening pain alone does not diagnose OIH: tolerance, withdrawal-associated pain and progression of the underlying condition must be distinguished. Its mechanism is not fully understood; NMDA-dependent and glial pathways are proposed contributors, not a fixed diagnostic mechanism in every patient. (c) Gabapentinoids bind α2δ subunits of voltage-gated calcium-channel complexes and modulate transmitter release; they do not simply block a calcium-channel pore. SNRIs alter monoaminergic modulation, including descending pain pathways. Spinal cord stimulation modulates spinal circuits, with mechanisms depending on the stimulation paradigm. These are condition- and patient-dependent approaches, not guaranteed reversal of central sensitization or treatment instructions. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system}.

FDA's safety and mechanism limits are described in its April 13, 2023 communication \citep{fda2023opioidoih}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: activity-dependent central sensitization and appropriately scoped non-opioid mechanisms; 2 points: conditional OIH, differential explanations for worsening pain and uncertainty about mechanism. 30. Critically evaluate the "connectome" project — the effort to map every synaptic connection in the human brain (~86 billion neurons, ~150 trillion synapses). Is the connectome sufficient to understand brain function, or is dynamic activity (electrophysiology, neuromodulation) equally important? Compare the completed *C. elegans* connectome (302 neurons, fully mapped) to our understanding of worm behavior — does knowing the wiring diagram explain the animal's behavior? Then apply the same evidence-boundary reasoning to a high-performance speech neuroprosthesis reported in one participant with paralysis.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** The connectome (a static wiring diagram) is necessary but not sufficient: the same circuit produces different outputs depending on dynamic activity, neuromodulatory state, and synaptic strength. The fully mapped \textit{C. elegans} connectome (302 neurons) still does not fully predict behavior, because neuromodulators (e.g., monoamines, peptides) reconfigure the functional circuit and synaptic weights/signs are not given by anatomy alone. So structure constrains but does not determine function — understanding the brain also requires electrophysiology and neuromodulation, making the human connectome a foundation rather than a complete explanation. A speech neuroprosthesis claim needs the same boundary: implanted electrodes directly record motor/speech-cortex activity during attempted speech, and a trained decoder converts those signals into text or avatar control in a defined task; the inference is decodable intention-related structure, not a general readout of private thought. Durable translation still depends on chronic signal stability, calibration burden, tissue response, infection risk, power, device replacement, performance outside the lab, consent, data ownership, privacy, autonomy, and access. See \cref{sec:unit_IX_nervous_system} and \citet{willett2023speechneuroprosthesis,metzger2023avatarneuroprosthesis}.
