1. Name the three major tissue systems in plants. Give one cell type belonging to each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** The three plant tissue systems are dermal, ground, and vascular. Dermal tissue is the outer protective covering; a representative cell type is the epidermal cell (e.g., a guard cell or root-hair cell). Ground tissue fills the interior; parenchyma (thin-walled, living, photosynthetic or storage cells) is a representative type, alongside collenchyma and sclerenchyma. Vascular tissue is the long-distance transport network; xylem (tracheids, vessel elements) and phloem (sieve-tube elements, companion cells) are its cell types. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

3. Define xylem and phloem. Which transports water and which transports sugars?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** Xylem is the vascular tissue that conducts water and dissolved minerals upward from roots to shoots; its conducting cells (tracheids and vessel elements) are dead and lignified at maturity. Phloem is the vascular tissue that transports sugars (sucrose at 0.3–0.9 M) plus amino acids and signaling molecules bidirectionally from sources to sinks; its sieve-tube elements remain alive (though they lack a nucleus). Xylem transports water; phloem transports sugars. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: xylem/phloem identities; 2 points: conducting-cell state and cargo. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

4. What are tracheids and vessel elements? How do they differ structurally?

<!-- SOLUTION

<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. Define water potential (Ψ). Write the equation Ψ = Ψ_s + Ψ_p.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** Water potential (Ψ, units MPa) is the free energy of water per unit volume relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure; water moves spontaneously from higher to lower Ψ. The simplified form is Ψ = Ψ_s + Ψ_p, where Ψ_s is the osmotic (solute) potential (Ψ_s = −iCRT, always ≤ 0) and Ψ_p is the pressure (turgor) potential (positive in turgid cells, negative in xylem under tension). The full four-component form is Ψ = Ψ_s + Ψ_p + Ψ_m + Ψ_g (matric and gravitational terms added). See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: water-potential components and units; 2 points: high-to-low direction. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

6. What is the Casparian strip? In which cell layer is it found and what does it do?

<!-- SOLUTION

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. Define transpiration. What drives transpiration in leaves?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** Transpiration is the evaporative loss of water vapor from the plant, mainly through open stomata in the leaf. It is driven by the steep water-potential (vapor-pressure) gradient between the leaf interior and the much drier atmosphere (Ψ ≈ −100 MPa at 50% RH): water evaporates from mesophyll cell-wall surfaces into the substomatal air space and diffuses out the stomata. This evaporation generates the tension that pulls the xylem water column upward. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: mesophyll evaporation; 2 points: gradient transmitted as xylem tension. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- assess: LO=LO7; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. What are stomata? What cells regulate their opening and closing?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Stomata are epidermal pores bounded by a pair of guard cells. Guard cells regulate aperture by changes in solute content and turgor. K$^+$ uptake and counterion/osmolyte accumulation lower osmotic potential; water enters when the total water-potential gradient favors influx, turgor rises and the pore widens. Counterions can include imported chloride or nitrate and malate accumulated through metabolism. Net solute loss during closure promotes water loss and reduced turgor. Transport and metabolic contributions depend on species and conditions; do not infer a universal channel, guard-cell shape or transport route. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: guard-cell turgor mechanism; 2 points: solute-flux/aperture directions. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

9. What is root pressure? Under what conditions does it occur?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** Root pressure is positive xylem pressure that can develop when ion accumulation lowers xylem solute potential and water enters osmotically, especially under well-watered, low-transpiration conditions. For the illustrative 0.05–0.5 MPa interval, ignoring flow resistance gives about 5.1–51 m (h=P/ρg with ρ=1000 kg/m³ and g=9.81 m/s²), not a universal 5 m maximum. Occurrence varies among species and conditions; embolism refilling requires species-specific evidence. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: pressure-head conversion; 2 points: species-specific limits. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.

<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. Describe the pressure-flow (Münch) hypothesis for phloem transport.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** The Münch pressure-flow hypothesis explains phloem transport as bulk flow driven by an osmotically generated turgor gradient. At a source (e.g., a mature leaf) sucrose is loaded into sieve tubes, lowering Ψ_s so water enters osmotically from adjacent xylem and raises turgor pressure. At a sink (root, fruit) sucrose is unloaded, Ψ_s rises, water leaves, and turgor falls. The resulting source-to-sink pressure difference drives bulk flow of phloem sap through the sieve-tube network; the direction is set by which tissue is loading vs. unloading. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: source loading/turgor; 2 points: source-to-sink pressure gradient. Award partial credit for correct intermediate reasoning; for quantitative work require a stated unit/boundary when applicable.
SOLUTION -->

11. A plant cell has Ψ_s = −0.9 MPa and Ψ_p = +0.4 MPa. Calculate its Ψ. If placed in a solution with Ψ = −0.3 MPa, will water enter or leave the cell? What will change as the cell reaches equilibrium?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** Use Ψ = Ψ_s + Ψ_p. Substituting: Ψ = (−0.9 MPa) + (+0.4 MPa) = **−0.5 MPa**. The external solution is at Ψ = −0.3 MPa, which is higher (less negative) than the cell's −0.5 MPa. Because water moves from higher to lower Ψ, **water enters the cell**. As water enters, the cell swells, Ψ_p rises (and Ψ_s becomes slightly less negative through dilution); equilibrium is reached when cell Ψ rises to −0.3 MPa to match the solution. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

**Scoring guide (4 points).** 2 points: −0.5 MPa and inward water flow; 2 points: equilibrium changes.

19. Halophytes (salt-tolerant plants) grow in saline soils (Ψ_s < −1.0 MPa). Explain three strategies: (a) accumulation of compatible solutes (proline) to lower Ψ_s; (b) ion compartmentalization into vacuoles (NHX1 Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter); (c) salt secretion via specialized salt glands.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** In saline soil (Ψ_s < −1.0 MPa) the soil Ψ is very negative, so halophytes must lower internal Ψ further to keep water moving inward while avoiding ion toxicity. (a) They synthesize compatible solutes such as proline and glycine betaine in the cytoplasm, lowering Ψ_s osmotically without poisoning enzymes. (b) They compartmentalise Na⁺ into the vacuole via the tonoplast NHX1 Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter, using the toxic ion as a cheap vacuolar osmoticum while protecting the cytosol. (c) Some species export salt through specialized epidermal salt glands or bladders, secreting Na⁺/Cl⁻ to the leaf surface. Together these maintain a favorable Ψ gradient for water uptake under salt stress. See \cref{sec:unit_VIII_plant_structure_and_water}.

<!-- SOLUTION
