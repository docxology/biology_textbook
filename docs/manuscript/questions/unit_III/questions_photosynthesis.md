# Questions — Photosynthesis {#sec:q_unit_III_photosynthesis .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Compute carbon-fixation requirements and explain a limiting factor.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Photon, ATP/NADPH, and Calvin-cycle stoichiometry.
- **Assessment alignment:** Representing and Describing Data, Statistical Tests and Data Analysis.
- **Misconception probe:** Plants do not eat sunlight; they use light energy to reduce carbon using electrons and enzymes.
- **Transfer product:** Transfer photosynthetic constraints to crops, algae, climate, or ecosystem productivity.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

## Questions 1–10: Core mechanisms {.unnumbered}

*This activity accompanies \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis} of the textbook — review that chapter before attempting the exercises below.*

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. Write the overall equation for photosynthesis. Which reactant provides O₂?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** Overall: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light → C6H12O6 + 6 O2 (ΔG ≈ +2,870 kJ/mol, an endergonic, light-driven reaction). The evolved O2 comes entirely from water, not CO2 — water is split by the oxygen-evolving complex of Photosystem II to supply electrons, protons, and O2. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. Locate photosynthesis: in which organelle and in which specific membrane compartment do the light reactions occur?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Photosynthesis occurs in the chloroplast (in plants and algae). The light reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane (with proton accumulation in the thylakoid lumen), while the Calvin cycle takes place in the surrounding stroma. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. What are photosystems I and II? What is the primary electron donor in each?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** Photosystems are pigment–protein reaction-center complexes in the thylakoid membrane. Photosystem II (P680) uses water as its primary electron donor (oxidising it via the oxygen-evolving complex to release O2). Photosystem I (P700) receives electrons from the cytochrome b6f chain via plastocyanin, and its excited reaction center donates electrons ultimately to NADP+. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Trace water-to-NADP+ electron transfer and explain the cytochrome b6f Q cycle contribution to lumenal protons.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** PSII extracts electrons from water; PQ carries them to b6f, plastocyanin to PSI, then ferredoxin/FNR reduce NADP+. PQH2 oxidation and Q cycling move protons toward the lumen, contributing PMF without producing NADPH at b6f itself. Award ordered path, proton location and NADPH-site distinction. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. What are the products of the light reactions passed to the Calvin cycle?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** The light reactions supply ATP and NADPH (plus reduced ferredoxin transiently) to the Calvin cycle in the stroma; O2 is released as a by-product and is not used by the cycle. ATP provides chemical energy and NADPH provides reducing power for CO2 fixation. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Name the three stages of the Calvin cycle (also called the C3 cycle or light-independent reactions).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** The three stages of the Calvin cycle are: (1) carboxylation — RuBisCO fixes CO2 onto ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) to form two 3-phosphoglycerate; (2) reduction — 3-PGA is phosphorylated (ATP) and reduced (NADPH) to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P); (3) regeneration — most G3P is used (with ATP) to regenerate RuBP. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is RuBisCO? What reaction does it catalyse and what is its substrate?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) is the Calvin-cycle enzyme that catalyses carboxylation: it adds CO2 to its substrate ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), producing two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate. It also has an oxygenase side-activity (using O2 instead of CO2) that initiates photorespiration. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO11; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. Distinguish C₃, C₄, and CAM plants. Give one example species of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** C3 plants fix CO2 through Rubisco in the Calvin cycle (e.g. wheat). In the common C4 pattern, initial PEPC fixation in mesophyll supplies four-carbon acids whose decarboxylation concentrates CO2 near bundle-sheath Rubisco (e.g. maize). In the canonical CAM diel pattern, nocturnal uptake supplies stored acids and daytime decarboxylation supports Rubisco (e.g. pineapple); expressed phases vary. C4 spatial and CAM temporal concentration can save water under specified conditions, but no universal WUE ranking follows without species, environment and denominator. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is photorespiration? Under what conditions is it maximized?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** Photorespiration begins when RuBisCO's oxygenase activity adds O2 (instead of CO2) to RuBP, making 2-phosphoglycolate that must be salvaged through the C2 pathway across chloroplast, peroxisome, and mitochondrion, releasing CO2 and consuming ATP with no sugar gain. It is maximized under high temperature, high light, and low CO2/high O2 (e.g. closed stomata in hot, dry conditions), where the oxygenase reaction is favored. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. What is the wavelength absorbed by chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Chlorophyll a absorbs maximally at roughly 430 nm (blue) and 662–680 nm (red). Chlorophyll b absorbs maximally at roughly 453 nm (blue) and 642 nm (red); it is an accessory pigment that broadens the harvested spectrum and funnels energy to chlorophyll a. Both reflect green light, giving plants their color. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->


## Questions 11–20: Mechanisms and evidence {.unnumbered}

<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
11. DCMU inhibits the QB acceptor site of Photosystem II. Predict effects of effective inhibition on O₂ evolution, linear-flow NADPH production, photophosphorylation and Calvin-cycle activity. Which conclusions additionally require dose, duration and recovery information?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** Effective PSII inhibition reduces linear electron flow and its associated oxygen evolution and NADPH production. It also reduces the proton-motive-force contribution of that flow. Residual cyclic electron flow can support ATP formation without net NADPH production; stored metabolites can transiently support downstream reactions. Carbon-fixation loss and whole-plant death depend on exposure, reserves and reversibility, rather than following unconditionally from target identity. Credit the direct linear-flow effects, ATP/NADPH distinction and conditional downstream outcome. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

12. Non-cyclic photophosphorylation produces both ATP and NADPH. Cyclic photophosphorylation produces primarily ATP. Explain the mechanism of cyclic flow (electron recirculates through Fd → PQ → Cyt b₆f → PSI) and when a cell uses cyclic vs non-cyclic flow.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Cyclic flow returns PSI-excited electrons from ferredoxin to the PQ/b6f route, adding PMF without net NADPH or O2. With12 H+/O2 and a c14 ring, LEF produces18/7 ATP per2 NADPH, ratio9/7≈1.286, below Calvin demand1.5. Cyclic flow can close the deficit. Award mechanism, ratio and assumptions;9:6 already equals3:2 and cannot exemplify a deficit. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.

<!-- assess: LO=LO13; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
13. A student growing *Arabidopsis thaliana* increases light intensity 10-fold. Initially photosynthetic rate rises, then plateaus. Explain the two-phase response: which reaction limits at low light (light reactions) and which limits at high light (Calvin cycle enzyme capacity)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q13, Application).** At low light the rate is light-limited: the light reactions cannot supply ATP/NADPH fast enough, so photosynthesis rises roughly linearly with intensity. As intensity increases the system becomes light-saturated: the rate plateaus because the Calvin cycle's enzymatic capacity (RuBisCO turnover, RuBP regeneration) and CO2 supply become limiting, not photon capture. Adding CO2 or more RuBisCO would raise the plateau; adding light would not — the predicted, testable signature of a Calvin-cycle (carbon/enzyme) limitation. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO11; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
14. C₄ plants (e.g., maize) separate initial CO₂ fixation (HCO₃⁻ → oxaloacetate, in mesophyll) from RuBisCO (in bundle sheath). Explain: (a) how this concentrates CO₂ around RuBisCO; (b) why it reduces photorespiration; (c) why C₄ plants outcompete C₃ plants in hot, sunny conditions.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q14, Application).** (a) In C4 plants PEP carboxylase fixes HCO3- to oxaloacetate → malate in mesophyll cells; malate moves to bundle-sheath cells and is decarboxylated, releasing CO2 right at RuBisCO (~10× above ambient). (b) High local CO2 outcompetes O2 at RuBisCO's active site, so the oxygenase reaction and photorespiration are nearly eliminated. (c) In hot, sunny, dry conditions C3 plants lose CO2 to high photorespiration and must keep stomata more open; C4 plants maintain high carboxylation with smaller stomatal opening, so they have higher water-use efficiency and outcompete C3 plants there. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO7; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
15. In ideal natural linear electron flow, explain why producing one O₂ and two NADPH requires four productive excitations at PSII and four at PSI. Distinguish this architectural photon count from a claim that one red photon has less energy than the water-to-NADP⁺ redox span.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** Oxidizing two H2O to one O2 supplies four electrons; reducing two NADP+ requires those four electrons. In the natural Z-scheme each electron undergoes one productive charge separation in PSII and another in PSI, giving four photons at each photosystem, eight total in ideal linear flow. This is a minimum for that two-photosystem architecture, not a universal lower bound from the raw redox span: a 700 nm photon has about 1.77 eV, exceeding a 1.14 eV-per-electron span. Reaction-center redox chemistry, intermediate steps and losses—not a false single-photon energy inequality—explain the natural two-excitation mechanism. Real absorbed-photon requirements increase with losses; cyclic flow may supply extra ATP. Award electron count, architectural assumption and correct energy comparison. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
16. Assume 25% of Rubisco turnovers are oxygenations and 75% are carboxylations. On a 100-mol turnover basis, each carboxylation fixes one CO$_2$ and canonical recycling releases one CO$_2$ per two oxygenations. What fraction of gross CO$_2$ fixation is released during recycling, and what net CO$_2$ gain remains before other respiratory losses?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** On the 100-mol turnover basis, 75 mol of carboxylations fix 75 mol CO$_2$, while 25 mol of oxygenations eventually release 12.5 mol CO$_2$ through canonical recycling. The loss relative to gross fixation is $12.5/75=1/6\approx16.7\%$; net gain is $75-12.5=62.5$ mol CO$_2$ before other respiratory losses. The supplied 25% describes a fraction of enzyme turnovers, not a fraction of gross fixed carbon lost. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO12; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
17. Antenna complexes (LHCI, LHCII) harvest photons and funnel excitation energy to reaction centers by resonance energy transfer (Förster transfer). Explain: (a) why antenna complexes increase efficiency; (b) why there is a risk of photodamage (singlet O₂ production) at high light; (c) what non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) does to protect the reaction centers.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Application).** (a) Antenna complexes (LHCI/LHCII) hold many pigments that absorb photons over a broad spectrum and funnel the excitation by Förster resonance/exciton transfer to the reaction center at ~95% efficiency, so each reaction center is fed far faster than it could collect light alone. (b) At high light, excitation arrives faster than photochemistry can use it; long-lived triplet chlorophyll forms and transfers energy to O2, generating damaging singlet oxygen. (c) Non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) — driven by lumen acidification, the xanthophyll/zeaxanthin cycle and PsbS — safely dissipates excess excitation as heat, protecting the reaction centers from photodamage. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

18. How do light-driven changes in stromal pH, Mg2+ and ferredoxin redox state coordinate Calvin-cycle activity? Distinguish thioredoxin regulation from Rubisco activase.

**Answer (Q18, Application).** Light alkalinizes stroma and raises free Mg2+; reduced ferredoxin reduces thioredoxin, activating selected Calvin enzymes by disulfide exchange. Rubisco activase instead uses ATP to remove inhibitory sugar phosphates; carbamylation and Mg2+ support Rubisco activation. Award pH/Mg2+, redox switch and distinct activase mechanism. 19. CAM (Crassulacean acid metabolism) plants open stomata primarily at night. Trace: (a) nocturnal CO₂ fixation into malate (by PEPC); (b) daytime malate decarboxylation to CO₂; (c) CO₂ fixation by RuBisCO; (d) the advantage in arid environments. Compare water-use efficiency (WUE) of CAM vs C₃ plants.

20. The chloroplast ATP synthase (CF₁F₀) is structurally similar to the mitochondrial ATP synthase. Compare: the direction of proton gradient (lumen to stroma vs IMS to matrix); whether CF₁F₀ can run in reverse (hydrolyse ATP to pump H⁺); and the regulatory role of the thiol-regulated γ-subunit in the dark vs light.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q20, Application).** Chloroplast CF1F0 and mitochondrial F1F0 share the rotary catalytic mechanism. Proton-gradient direction differs: in chloroplasts protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen and flow out to the stroma to make ATP, whereas in mitochondria protons flow from the intermembrane space into the matrix. CF1F0 can in principle run in reverse (ATP hydrolysis pumping H+), but the chloroplast enzyme is held off in the dark by a thiol-regulated γ-subunit: in the light, reduced thioredoxin (and the proton gradient) activates it for ATP synthesis; in the dark the oxidised γ-subunit inhibits it to prevent wasteful ATP hydrolysis. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

22. Rising CO₂ atmospheric concentrations (currently ~420 ppm) should theoretically increase C₃ photosynthesis (CO₂ fertilization effect) but FACE (Free Air CO₂ Enrichment) experiments show attenuated responses. Evaluate three mechanisms of "photosynthetic downregulation" under elevated CO₂ and assess the net impact on crop yields.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q22, Synthesis).** FACE experiments show smaller-than-predicted C3 gains under elevated CO2 because of photosynthetic downregulation: (1) acclimation — sustained sugar accumulation represses RuBisCO/photosynthetic gene expression and lowers RuBisCO content; (2) sink limitation — if growth/storage capacity does not increase, end-product feedback (Pi limitation) caps the rate; (3) co-limitation by nitrogen and water — extra CO2 cannot be used without proportional N for RuBisCO and adequate water/nutrients. Net crop-yield effects are real but modest (often well below the theoretical CO2-fertilization expectation) and are further offset by heat and drought, so the simple fertilization prediction should not be treated as settled. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

23. Distinguish Rubisco CO2 affinity, catalytic turnover and CO2/O2 specificity. 24. Shade-adapted plants have higher LHCII content and lower PSII/PSI ratios than sun plants. Evaluate the molecular mechanisms of this acclimation: how does excess excitation pressure in low light signal LHCII assembly vs LHCII disassembly in high light? What role does STN7 kinase (LHCII phosphorylation) play in state transitions?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q24, Synthesis).** Acclimation is driven by the redox state of the plastoquinone pool sensing excitation balance. In low light/shade, cells build more LHCII and lower the PSII:PSI ratio to maximize capture. Imbalanced excitation that over-reduces the PQ pool activates the STN7 kinase, which phosphorylates LHCII so it migrates from PSII to PSI (state transition, redistributing energy); when PSI is over-excited and the PQ pool is oxidised, STN7 is inactive and the PPH1/TAP38 phosphatase dephosphorylates LHCII, returning it to PSII. Longer-term, this redox signaling also adjusts photosystem stoichiometry and antenna size between sun and shade plants. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

25. Oxygenic photosynthesis evolved ~2.7 billion years ago and fundamentally transformed Earth's atmosphere. Evaluate the geochemical evidence (banded iron formations, the Great Oxidation Event) for the timing of oxygenic photosynthesis. What was the ecological impact on anaerobic life, and how did some lineages evolve oxygen tolerance?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q25, Synthesis).** Oxygenic photosynthesis arose in cyanobacteria, with the Great Oxidation Event ~2.4 Ga. Geochemical evidence: banded iron formations record episodic O2 oxidising dissolved Fe2+ to insoluble Fe3+ before free O2 accumulated; the disappearance of detrital pyrite/uraninite, the rise of red beds, and the loss of mass-independent sulfur isotope fractionation mark the GOE. Ecologically, O2 was toxic to obligate anaerobes, driving them to anoxic refugia (a mass extinction for some lineages) while others evolved oxygen tolerance — antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase) and ultimately aerobic respiration, which exploited O2 as a high-yield electron acceptor. 27. Photoinhibition occurs when PSII is irreversibly damaged by excess light. The D1 protein of PSII is a site of photodamage and must be replaced every ~30 minutes under high light. Evaluate the molecular mechanism of D1 turnover (degradation by FtsH protease, ribosomal de novo synthesis, PSII reassembly in thylakoid membrane) and explain why this turnover rate limits maximal photosynthetic productivity.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q27, Synthesis).** High light over-excites PSII; reactive oxygen species (notably singlet O2) damage the D1 reaction-center protein, which must be replaced every ~30 minutes — the fastest protein turnover known. The PSII-repair cycle: damaged PSII is partly disassembled and moved from grana to stroma lamellae, D1 is selectively degraded by the FtsH (and Deg) proteases, a new D1 is synthesized by thylakoid-bound ribosomes and co-translationally inserted, then PSII is reassembled and the Mn4CaO5 cluster photoactivated. Because repair has a finite maximal rate, when photodamage outpaces it net photosynthesis declines (chronic photoinhibition), capping maximal productivity under intense light. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
SOLUTION -->

28. Horizontal gene transfer of photosynthesis genes between cyanobacteria and early eukaryotes (primary endosymbiosis) versus between non-photosynthetic organisms and photosynthetic ones (secondary endosymbiosis) produced the diverse eukaryotic algal groups. Evaluate the complexity of gene transfer that was required to set up a functional chloroplast, focusing on the challenge of targeting nuclear-encoded proteins to the chloroplast (TIC/TOC translocon import).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q28, Synthesis).** Establishing a functional chloroplast required far more than transferring photosynthesis genes. After primary endosymbiosis of a cyanobacterium, the bulk of the endosymbiont genome was lost or transferred to the host nucleus (endosymbiotic gene transfer), so ~95% of chloroplast proteins are now nuclear-encoded and must be re-imported. 29. Phytoplankton (mainly diatoms, dinoflagellates, cyanobacteria) account for ~50% of global CO₂ fixation despite having about 1% of terrestrial plant biomass. Evaluate what properties of aquatic photosynthesis explain this high per-mass productivity, and assess the feasibility of enhancing ocean photosynthesis (e.g., iron fertilization) as a climate change mitigation strategy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q29, Synthesis).** Phytoplankton fix ~50% of global CO2 with ~1% of photosynthetic biomass because of extremely high turnover, not standing stock: short cell-cycle times, no investment in non-photosynthetic structural tissue (roots, wood), efficient nutrient and light capture in the mixed layer, and many have CO2-concentrating mechanisms (carboxysomes/pyrenoids) that suppress photorespiration. Iron fertilization can transiently bloom phytoplankton in iron-limited (HNLC) oceans, but its value as durable carbon sequestration is doubtful: most fixed carbon is rapidly respired and recycled, export to the deep ocean is low and hard to verify, and ecological side-effects (toxic blooms, deoxygenation, nutrient robbing) are significant — so it is not an established climate solution. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.

30. Cyanobacteria already co-locate photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport in thylakoid membranes. Using this natural counterexample, evaluate the distinct proposal to combine the two systems in one engineered eukaryotic organelle. Identify shared carriers, regulatory challenges and measurements needed to demonstrate useful coupled function rather than infer impossibility from co-location alone.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** General co-location is not an unmet feasibility problem: cyanobacterial thylakoids contain photosystems and respiratory complexes, sharing cytochrome b6f, plastoquinone and plastocyanin/cytochrome carriers. Live-cell localization and electron-transport work in Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 links redox-regulated redistribution of NDH-1/SDH to electron partitioning between PSI and terminal oxidases \citep{Liu2012ElectronRoutes}. Engineering a particular eukaryotic organelle additionally requires correct membrane insertion/orientation, compatible cofactors, controlled redox partitioning and ROS protection, and balanced ATP/reductant demand. The cyanobacterial result refutes a blanket thermodynamic impossibility but does not demonstrate a working engineered organelle. Full credit distinguishes that evidence from the proposed engineering task. See \cref{sec:unit_III_photosynthesis}.
