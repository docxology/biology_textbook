# Questions — Metabolic Integration and Regulation {#sec:q_unit_III_metabolic_integration .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Predict how a change in substrate, hormone, or enzyme activity redirects flux.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Energy charge, control points, and pathway-flux comparisons.
- **Assessment alignment:** Representing and Describing Data, Statistical Tests and Data Analysis.
- **Misconception probe:** A pathway is not a one-way assembly line; reversibility and regulation define the real route.
- **Transfer product:** Apply metabolic network reasoning to diabetes, fasting, exercise, or cancer metabolism.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

## Questions 1–10: Core mechanisms {.unnumbered}

*This activity accompanies \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration} of the textbook — review that chapter before attempting the exercises below.*

<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. What does "metabolic integration" mean? Give one example of how carbohydrate and lipid metabolism are connected.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** Metabolic integration coordinates pathways and organs so fuel supply matches changing demand. For example, fed-state insulin favors muscle/adipose glucose uptake and liver storage or lipogenesis, linking carbohydrate supply to lipid synthesis. During fasting, lower insulin permits adipose fatty-acid release, while hepatic glucagon signaling favors liver glucose output and ketogenesis. These are tissue-dependent programs, not evidence that glucagon directly stimulates human adipose lipolysis. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. Define anabolism and catabolism. Give one example of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Catabolism breaks complex molecules into simpler ones, releasing usable energy or reducing power (for example, glucose oxidation through glycolysis and the TCA cycle). Anabolism builds molecules using energy and reducing power (for example, fatty-acid synthesis). Reciprocal regulation can limit wasteful opposing fluxes, but anabolic and catabolic processes can coexist across tissues, compartments and regulated substrate cycles. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. What is the role of AMP:ATP ratio as a metabolic signal? Which enzyme is the key AMP sensor?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** A rising AMP:ATP (and ADP:ATP) ratio signals energy deficit, allosterically and covalently shifting metabolism toward ATP-producing catabolism. The key AMP sensor is AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK): AMP binding (and LKB1 phosphorylation) activates it when cellular energy charge falls. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO9; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. What is AMPK? Describe two metabolic processes it activates and two it inhibits.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** AMPK is the cell's low-energy sensor, activated by a high AMP:ATP ratio. It activates catabolic, ATP-generating processes — e.g. glucose uptake (GLUT4) and fatty acid oxidation (by inhibiting acetyl-CoA carboxylase, lowering malonyl-CoA). It inhibits anabolic, ATP-consuming processes — e.g. fatty acid/cholesterol synthesis (ACC, HMG-CoA reductase) and mTORC1-driven protein synthesis. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. What is mTOR? What signals activate it, and what anabolic processes does it promote?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin), as mTORC1, is the cell's nutrient/growth sensor. It is activated by amino acids (via Rag GTPases and the Ragulator at the lysosome), insulin/growth-factor signaling through PI3K–Akt, and a high energy charge. It promotes anabolism: protein synthesis (S6K1, 4E-BP1), lipid and nucleotide synthesis, and ribosome biogenesis, while suppressing autophagy. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Name the organs that play a primary role in glucose homeostasis after a meal.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** After a meal the principal organs are the pancreas (β-cells secrete insulin in response to rising glucose), the liver (takes up glucose, stores glycogen, runs lipogenesis), skeletal muscle (insulin-stimulated GLUT4 glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis), and adipose tissue (glucose uptake and triacylglycerol storage). See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is the metabolic role of the liver in the fed vs fasted state?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** Fed state: high insulin/low glucagon — the liver takes up glucose, makes glycogen and fatty acids (lipogenesis), and suppresses glucose output. Fasted state: low insulin/high glucagon — the liver releases glucose by glycogenolysis then gluconeogenesis, and produces ketone bodies from fatty-acid-derived acetyl-CoA to fuel peripheral tissues and brain. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. Define the Cori cycle. Which two organs are involved, and which molecule cycles between them?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** The Cori cycle links exercising/anaerobic muscle and the liver. Muscle produces lactate by glycolysis; lactate travels to the liver and is converted back to glucose by gluconeogenesis (net cost ~6 ATP in liver), and that glucose returns to muscle. Lactate is the molecule that cycles between the two organs. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is the glucose-fatty acid cycle (Randle cycle)? What is the primary substrate competition it describes?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** The Randle (glucose–fatty acid) cycle describes reciprocal substrate competition in heart and skeletal muscle: increased fatty acid oxidation raises acetyl-CoA and citrate, inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase and PFK-1, so glucose oxidation falls — and conversely high glucose suppresses fatty acid oxidation. The core competition is between glucose and fatty acids as oxidative fuels. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO12; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. Define metabolic syndrome. List four diagnostic criteria.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of cardiometabolic risk factors driven by insulin resistance and central adiposity. Four diagnostic criteria (any three define it): central/abdominal obesity (large waist circumference), elevated fasting glucose (or insulin resistance), elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL cholesterol, and elevated blood pressure. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->


## Questions 11–20: Mechanisms and evidence {.unnumbered}

<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
11. After a high-carbohydrate meal, blood glucose rises. Trace the hormonal and metabolic responses in: (a) the pancreas; (b) the liver; (c) skeletal muscle; (d) adipose tissue — over the 2 hours post-meal.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** (a) Pancreas: rising glucose promotes beta-cell insulin secretion and, in the stated carbohydrate-fed context, reduced glucagon signaling. (b) Liver: insulin favors glycogen storage and lipogenesis while suppressing gluconeogenic gene expression and flux in a responsive liver; this does not establish zero gluconeogenesis. (c) Skeletal muscle: insulin–PI3K–Akt promotes GLUT4 translocation, glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis. (d) Adipose tissue: GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake, lipoprotein-lipase-mediated fatty-acid uptake and triacylglycerol storage accompany suppression of lipolysis. These storage responses can lower blood glucose over the stated interval; the time course depends on the meal, insulin sensitivity and other conditions. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
12. Compare early fasting with prolonged starvation: (a) which stored carbohydrate depot declines first; (b) where does gluconeogenesis contribute; (c) what does Owen et al.'s study establish about brain ketone use, and what timing or universal percentage does it not establish?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** (a) Liver glycogen declines first, with timing dependent on starting stores and demand. (b) Gluconeogenesis from lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids increases as glycogen contribution falls; renal contribution increases in prolonged fasting. (c) Ketone production and brain use increase progressively over days to weeks, reducing but not eliminating glucose demand. Owen et al. studied cerebral substrate exchange in three obese patients after 5–6 weeks of starvation and found ketones were predominant brain fuel \citep{Owen1967}; that is not evidence for 75% brain fuel after three days. Full credit separates the qualitative adaptation from the study-specific duration/population. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
13. Insulin stimulates GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane of muscle and adipose cells via PI3K-Akt-AS160 pathway. Explain the molecular cascade from insulin receptor activation to GLUT4 vesicle exocytosis. What happens to GLUT4 when insulin is removed?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q13, Application).** Insulin binds its receptor tyrosine kinase → receptor autophosphorylation → IRS-1 phosphorylation → PI3K → PIP3 → PDK1/Akt activation → Akt phosphorylates AS160 (TBC1D4), relieving its Rab-GAP activity → active Rab10 permits GLUT4 storage vesicles to dock and fuse via SNAREs at the plasma membrane, increasing glucose uptake. When insulin is removed, the cascade reverses, GLUT4 is endocytosed and recycled back to intracellular vesicles, and glucose uptake falls. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
14. PFK-1 (phosphofructokinase-1) is a key regulatory enzyme of glycolysis. It is activated by AMP, ADP, and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F-2,6-BP) and inhibited by ATP, citrate, and H⁺. Explain how each regulator makes physiological sense in terms of energy status signaling.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q14, Application).** PFK-1 catalyses the committed glycolytic step and is tuned to energy status. AMP and ADP rise when ATP is low and activate PFK-1 (more glycolysis to make ATP). Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, high when insulin/fed signaling raises glycolysis, is a potent activator. ATP (substrate at high concentration) and citrate (signaling abundant TCA fuel) inhibit it, and H+ inhibits it to prevent excessive lactic acid in hypoxia — each regulator matches glycolytic flux to need. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO12; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
15. A patient with type 2 diabetes has high fasting glucose and insulin. Explain: (a) plausible tissue-specific insulin-resistance mechanisms; (b) why hepatic glucose production can remain high; (c) how chronic hyperglycemia affects HbA1c and its interpretation; (d) why metformin-induced AMPK activation alone does not identify the necessary mechanism of glucose lowering.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** (a) Lipid intermediates, inflammatory signaling and altered proximal insulin signaling can impair PI3K–Akt responses, with tissue-specific contributions; these observations do not identify one universal IRS-1 phosphorylation site or cause. In muscle and adipose cells, impaired signaling can reduce GLUT4 translocation. (b) Inadequate suppression of hepatic glucose production can involve impaired FOXO1 regulation, glucagon and substrate supply. (c) Hemoglobin glycation generally increases with sustained hyperglycemia, but HbA1c is a time-weighted indicator affected by red-cell survival and hemoglobin variants, not an exact glucose concentration. (d) Metformin still inhibited glucose production in the studied AMPK-deficient mouse preparations \citep{foretz2010metformin}; therefore AMPK activation is not sufficient evidence that AMPK is necessary for that effect. This does not establish a unique universal human mechanism. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO7; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
16. Calculate ATP from palmitoyl-CoA: 7 NADH and 7 Q-linked electron pairs from beta-oxidation, plus 8 acetyl-CoA yielding 10 ATP each. Use P/O 2.5 and 1.5. Contrast free palmitate.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** 7×2.5+7×1.5+8×10=108 ATP from activated palmitoyl-CoA. Free palmitate activation costs 2 ATP equivalents (ATP→AMP+PPi), giving 106. Award products, arithmetic and starting substrate; do not subtract activation twice. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->

17. Pools change from ATP3, ADP1, AMP0.2 mM to ATP1, ADP2, AMP1 mM. Calculate energy charge and explain how AMPK senses the change.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Application).** EC=(ATP+0.5ADP)/(ATP+ADP+AMP): 0.8333 then 0.5. AMP/ADP binding to γ favors activation or maintenance of α Thr172 phosphorylation; β scaffolds the complex. Award both calculations, one α/β/γ stoichiometry and the limit that EC is not a kinase-activity measurement. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
18. In the selected hepatic pathway, trace glucagon binding → Gs → cAMP → PKA → CREB/co-activator regulation → increased PEPCK and G6Pase gene transcription. How does insulin–PI3K–Akt oppose this transcriptional response through FoxO1 phosphorylation and localization, and why does that not establish zero hepatic glucose output?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q18, Application).** Glucagon binds its Gs-coupled receptor → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA. Phosphorylated CREB and appropriate co-activators can promote PEPCK and glucose-6-phosphatase expression, contributing to gluconeogenic capacity; PKA also supports acute hepatic glycogenolysis. Insulin–PI3K–Akt phosphorylates FoxO1, favoring nuclear exclusion and reducing its contribution to gluconeogenic gene expression. Akt is not itself a cAMP-degrading enzyme. The transcriptional contrast does not quantify instantaneous gluconeogenic flux or total hepatic glucose output, which also depends on substrate supply, other regulators and glycogenolysis. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO13; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
19. Enzyme abundance rises from 100 to 110 units while steady flux rises from 50 to 52 µmol/min. Estimate C_E^J≈(ΔJ/J)/(ΔE/E). Does substrate saturation imply zero enzyme control?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** C=(2/50)/(10/100)=0.4, a local finite-difference estimate. Saturation concerns substrate elasticity, not enzyme-abundance control: an isolated saturated enzyme can have C=1. Award calculation, locality and elasticity/control distinction. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->

20. Explain reciprocal regulation of fatty-acid synthesis and mitochondrial beta-oxidation within a tissue. Why is it not universal mutual exclusion throughout a body?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q20, Application).** ACC-derived malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT1, reducing long-chain acyl import while cytosolic synthesis is favored. Tissue, compartment and timing matter: one organ can synthesize while another oxidizes, and residual opposing fluxes can coexist. Award malonyl-CoA, CPT1, compartment and an explicit nonabsolute boundary. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.

22. A substrate cycle has forward/reverse fluxes12/10 µmol/min, then13/9 after signaling. Find net flux and relative changes; explain amplification and energy cost.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q22, Application).** Net flux doubles from 2 to 4 µmol/min while forward flux rises 8.3% and reverse falls 10%. Reciprocal regulation amplifies the net response; opposing reactions can consume high-energy phosphate and dissipate heat. Award arithmetic, fractional changes, control advantage and energetic cost. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Create; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
23. Foretz et al. observed metformin-dependent inhibition of glucose production in studied AMPK-deficient mouse preparations \citep{foretz2010metformin}. Design an experiment separating AMPK dependence, acute adenylate/flux effects and slower transcriptional effects. Include CRTC2 (the CREB co-activator, historically TORC2, not mTORC2), genetic controls, time-resolved readouts and limits of inhibitor-based inference.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q23, Synthesis).** Compare independent wild-type and AMPK α1/α2-deficient hepatocyte preparations with a matched genetic rescue, vehicle and a justified AMPK-directed perturbation. Do not treat AICAR or metformin as selective proof of AMPK causation. Measure glucose-production rate normalized to viable cells, ATP/ADP/AMP, AMPK activity or substrate phosphorylation, CRTC2 phosphorylation/localization, and PEPCK/G6Pase RNA/protein over acute and later intervals with matched substrates and exposure. A transcription-blocking contrast needs toxicity controls; an appropriate CRTC2 phospho-mutant tests that branch rather than every drug effect. Acute output suppression without corresponding RNA change argues against a solely transcriptional explanation. Persistence in AMPK-deficient cells shows AMPK is not necessary under those tested conditions; rescue, target engagement and viability distinguish adaptation or nonspecific toxicity. It does not identify the sole alternative mechanism or prove a human clinical effect. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->

24. Evaluate evidence for a change in fuel use during a specified feeding/fasting or substrate challenge. Distinguish whole-body RER from cellular oxygen-consumption and extracellular-acidification measurements. Why can neither a disease label nor one extracellular-flux trace establish a uniform loss of metabolic flexibility in type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance or cancer?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q24, Synthesis).** Define the preparation and perturbation before judging flexibility. For whole-body gas exchange, match diet, activity and timing and state the assumptions connecting RER with substrate utilization; net synthesis and other pathways can confound simple oxidation estimates \citep{frayn1983substrateoxidation}. For cells, use matched cell type, substrates, exposure and normalization, with viability and relevant inhibitor controls. An extracellular-flux analyzer records oxygen consumption and acidification, not a unique fuel-oxidation fraction: respiratory CO$_2$ can contribute to acidification, so ECAR is not identical to glycolytic lactate flux \citep{mookerjee2015acidproduction}. Add substrate-specific uptake/oxidation or isotope-tracing evidence when attributing fuel choice. Insulin-resistant and cancer preparations can differ by genotype and conditions; high glycolysis does not imply absent mitochondrial oxidation. Credit a discriminating comparison and explicit readout limits, not a fixed diagnostic RQ value. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->

25. The metabolic role of the gut microbiome is increasingly recognized: SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate) produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber signal to host cells. Evaluate how propionate signals to the liver (inhibiting gluconeogenesis) and butyrate signals to colonocytes (primary fuel) and immune cells (HDAC inhibition, regulatory T cell induction).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q25, Synthesis).** Gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber to short-chain fatty acids that act as inter-organ signals. Propionate is absorbed and reaches the liver, where it signals (via FFAR3/GPR41 and as a gluconeogenic/intestinal-gluconeogenesis cue) to restrain hepatic glucose output and improve glucose homeostasis. Butyrate is the primary oxidative fuel for colonocytes and, in immune cells, acts as an HDAC inhibitor that promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation and an anti-inflammatory tone — linking the microbiome to host energy balance and immunometabolism. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.

26. Compare 16:8 time-restricted eating with continuous energy restriction; distinguish both from a 5:2 regimen (two restricted days per week) and alternate-day fasting. Propose three testable metabolic mechanisms, then design a comparison that could establish benefit beyond total energy intake. No clinical outcome data are supplied, so state which efficacy conclusions are unavailable.

<!-- SOLUTION

27. Some cancer cells depend strongly on glutamine for anaplerosis, while others use glucose-derived pyruvate carboxylation — replenishing TCA cycle intermediates for biosynthesis. Evaluate: (a) which TCA intermediate is most depleted in rapidly proliferating cells; (b) how glutamine enters the TCA cycle (via glutaminase → glutamate → α-ketoglutarate); (c) why targeting glutamine metabolism (CB-839, a glutaminase inhibitor) might be selectively toxic to cancer cells.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q27, Synthesis).** (a) Oxaloacetate (and α-ketoglutarate-derived intermediates) is most depleted because biosynthesis siphons TCA intermediates ('cataplerosis'). (b) Glutamine enters via glutaminase → glutamate → (transaminase/glutamate dehydrogenase) → α-ketoglutarate, which replenishes the cycle (anaplerosis) for energy and biosynthetic precursors. (c) Many proliferating tumors are 'glutamine-addicted' for anaplerosis and redox/biosynthesis, so a glutaminase inhibitor (CB-839/telaglenastat) can be more toxic to them than to normal tissue that has flexible anaplerotic sources — though efficacy is context-dependent and resistance can arise. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
SOLUTION -->

28. Evaluate the concept of "metaflammation" — the chronic low-grade inflammation seen in obesity. Propose a mechanistic link from adipocyte lipid overload → ER stress → NF-κB activation → pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNF-α, IL-6) secretion → insulin resistance in muscle and liver. At which step would you intervene pharmacologically?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q28, Synthesis).** Metaflammation is chronic low-grade inflammation from nutrient overload. Mechanistic chain: adipocyte lipid overload and ectopic lipid → ER stress and excess saturated FFAs/diacylglycerol → activation of JNK and IKKβ/NF-κB → secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1 with macrophage infiltration → these cytokines drive serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 in muscle and liver, impairing PI3K–Akt and causing insulin resistance. 29. Evaluate the historical "two-hit" account of fatty liver disease: lipid accumulation followed by oxidative/inflammatory injury. Why need these processes not form a mandatory sequence, and what comparative evidence would establish benefit from a treatment targeting one or several mechanisms?

<!-- SOLUTION

30. Critically evaluate the use of incretin therapies (semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist) for obesity treatment. What are the distinct mechanisms by which GLP-1 agonists reduce appetite (hypothalamic signaling), slow gastric emptying, and promote pancreatic β-cell survival? What are the metabolic consequences of rapid weight loss induced by these drugs (muscle loss, bone density) and how should therapy be managed?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** GLP-1 receptor agonists act through distinct mechanisms: in the hypothalamus they activate POMC/CART and suppress NPY/AgRP neurons to reduce appetite; in the gut they slow gastric emptying, prolonging satiety; and on pancreatic β-cells they enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and promote β-cell survival/function. Management should pair therapy with adequate protein intake and resistance exercise, gradual titration to limit GI effects, and monitoring of body composition and bone health, with attention to weight regain on discontinuation. See \cref{sec:unit_III_metabolic_integration}.
