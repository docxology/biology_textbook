# Questions — Bioenergetics and Cellular Respiration {#sec:q_unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Calculate an energy yield or redox consequence and connect it to cellular work.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Delta G, ATP yield, and electron-carrier accounting.
- **Assessment alignment:** Representing and Describing Data, Statistical Tests and Data Analysis.
- **Misconception probe:** ATP is not stored energy in a vague sense; it is a coupling currency with defined reaction chemistry.
- **Transfer product:** Transfer energy accounting to exercise, fermentation, hypoxia, and mitochondrial disease.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

## Questions 1–10: Core mechanisms {.unnumbered}

*This activity accompanies \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration} of the textbook — review that chapter before attempting the exercises below.*

<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. Define free energy (G). What does a negative ΔG indicate about a reaction?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** Free energy (G) is the Gibbs free energy, the portion of a system's energy available to do useful work at constant temperature and pressure. A negative ΔG (exergonic) indicates a spontaneous reaction that releases free energy; a positive ΔG (endergonic) requires an energy input. Cells couple endergonic reactions (e.g. biosynthesis) to highly exergonic ones such as ATP hydrolysis to drive them forward. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. Write the overall equation for aerobic cellular respiration.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Aerobic respiration: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, with ΔG°' ≈ −2,870 kJ/mol. Glucose is fully oxidised to CO2, O2 is the terminal electron acceptor reduced to water, and the released free energy is captured as ~30–32 ATP per glucose. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. What is ATP? Describe terminal-phosphate hydrolysis and explain why bond breaking alone does not release its net free energy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** ATP (adenosine triphosphate) contains adenosine and three phosphates, including two phosphoanhydride linkages. For ATP + H$_2$O → ADP + P$_i$, a conventional standard transformed reference is approximately $-30.5$ kJ/mol; actual cellular $\Delta G$ depends on the normalized reactant/product quotient and thermodynamic conditions. Hydrolysis is a complete reaction involving bond breaking, bond formation and product stabilization through electrostatic, resonance and solvation effects—not release of energy by breaking a bond alone. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Name the four stages of aerobic respiration and state where each occurs in the eukaryotic cell.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** The four stages are: (1) glycolysis — cytoplasm; (2) pyruvate decarboxylation (PDC) — mitochondrial matrix; (3) the citric acid (Krebs/TCA) cycle — mitochondrial matrix; (4) the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation — inner mitochondrial membrane. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
5. How many ATP are produced by substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Application).** Glycolysis produces 4 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation but consumes 2 ATP in the investment phase, for a net of 2 ATP per glucose by substrate-level phosphorylation (steps 7 and 10, phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase). It also yields 2 net NADH. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. What is the purpose of the preparatory steps (pyruvate decarboxylation) before the Krebs cycle?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** Pyruvate decarboxylation links glycolysis to the TCA cycle. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex oxidatively decarboxylates each pyruvate (3C), releasing one CO2, reducing one NAD+ to NADH, and attaching the remaining 2-carbon acetyl group to coenzyme A to form acetyl-CoA. This produces 2 NADH and 2 CO2 per glucose and feeds acetyl-CoA into the cycle. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is the electron transport chain? Name the four major protein complexes.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** The electron transport chain is a series of redox carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane that pass electrons from NADH and FADH2 to O2, using the released energy to pump protons and build the proton-motive force. The four major complexes are Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), Complex III (cytochrome bc1, the Q cycle), and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO7; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. What is the chemiosmotic theory? Who proposed it and when?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** The chemiosmotic theory states that electron transport pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, creating an electrochemical proton gradient (proton-motive force) whose energy drives ATP synthesis by ATP synthase. It was proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961 and earned him the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. What is fermentation? Name two types and the products of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** In the illustrated lactate and ethanol pathways, terminal reactions regenerate NAD+ from NADH so glycolysis can continue: pyruvate is reduced to lactate, or converted through acetaldehyde to ethanol plus CO2. These terminal redox steps add no ATP; the net two ATP per glucose comes from glycolysis. This accounting is specific to the named pathways, not a claim that every fermentative metabolism lacks additional ATP-producing reactions. Credit products, redox regeneration and pathway-specific ATP scope. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO9; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. What is the difference between substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** In substrate-level phosphorylation a high-energy phosphate is transferred directly from a substrate to ADP by an enzyme (glycolysis steps 7 and 10, and the TCA GTP step); it is fast, O2-independent, and yields little ATP. In oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthase uses the proton-motive force generated by the electron transport chain; it requires O2 and supplies ~28 of the ~32 ATP per glucose. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->


## Questions 11–20: Mechanisms and evidence {.unnumbered}

<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
11. Calculate the theoretical maximum ATP yield from one glucose molecule in aerobic respiration. How does the actual yield (~30 ATP) differ from the textbook maximum (~38), and why?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** Theoretical yield: 2 (glycolysis SLP) + 2 (TCA GTP) + 10 NADH × 2.5 + 2 FADH2 × 1.5 ≈ 32 ATP using modern P/O ratios (the classic '38' assumed 3 ATP/NADH and 2 ATP/FADH2). The actual ~30 is lower because the malate-aspartate shuttle vs glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle changes the value of cytosolic NADH (2.5 vs 1.5 ATP each), and because proton leak and the cost of importing Pi/ADP siphon off part of the proton-motive force. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
12. In anaerobic fermentation, yeast converts pyruvate to ethanol + CO₂. Explain why this regeneration of NAD⁺ is essential for continued glycolysis, even though no ATP is produced in the process.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Glycolysis nets 2 ATP but reduces 2 NAD+ to NADH at the GAPDH step. Cytosolic NAD+ is present in only catalytic amounts, so if NADH is not reoxidised, GAPDH stalls and glycolysis (and its 2 ATP) halts. Converting pyruvate to ethanol + CO2 reoxidises NADH back to NAD+, sustaining flux through glycolysis even though the fermentation step itself makes no ATP. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
13. Cyanide (CN⁻) blocks Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). Predict the consequences for: (a) proton gradient; (b) NADH/NAD⁺ ratio; (c) ATP production; (d) O₂ consumption; (e) lactic acid production.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q13, Application).** Cyanide blocks Complex IV, so electrons cannot reach O2. (a) Proton pumping stops, so the proton gradient collapses; (b) carriers stay reduced, so NADH/NAD+ rises sharply; (c) oxidative phosphorylation ATP production stops; (d) O2 consumption falls to near zero; (e) cells switch to fermentation to regenerate NAD+, so lactic acid production rises. The result is a lethal histotoxic hypoxia. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO14; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
14. Uncoupling proteins (UCPs) such as thermogenin in brown adipose tissue dissipate the proton gradient as heat rather than driving ATP synthesis. Explain the physiological role of this process and calculate: if 50% of the proton gradient is uncoupled, what happens to O₂ consumption vs ATP output?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q14, Application).** Uncoupling proteins (e.g. thermogenin/UCP1 in brown adipose tissue) let protons re-enter the matrix bypassing ATP synthase, so the gradient's energy is released as heat — important for non-shivering thermogenesis in neonates and cold-adapted mammals. Because the gradient is continually dissipated, electron transport speeds up: O2 consumption rises while ATP output falls. With 50% of the gradient uncoupled, roughly half the proton flux makes heat instead of ATP, so ATP yield per O2 drops by about half while total O2 consumption increases. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
15. Glycolysis includes an irreversible phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) step. PFK-1 is allosterically activated by AMP. ADP, and F-2,6-BP, and inhibited by ATP and citrate. Explain how this regulation matches glycolytic rate to cellular energy status.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** PFK-1 catalyses the committed, irreversible step of glycolysis. When energy is low, AMP and ADP rise and F-2,6-BP increases, allosterically activating PFK-1 so glycolysis speeds up to make ATP. When energy is plentiful, ATP and citrate accumulate and inhibit PFK-1, slowing glycolysis. This makes glycolytic flux track cellular energy charge moment to moment. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO14; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
16. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is thermogenic. Compare the metabolic activity of BAT vs white adipose tissue (WAT): what substrate do they preferentially oxidise, how do they regulate UCP1 expression (adrenergic → cAMP → PKA → lipolysis → FA activation of UCP1), and what clinical interest does BAT activation generate?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** Brown adipose tissue oxidises fatty acids primarily to drive UCP1-mediated heat production; white adipose tissue stores triacylglycerol and oxidises little itself. In BAT, cold/adrenergic signaling → β-adrenergic receptor → cAMP → PKA → lipolysis → free fatty acids that both fuel and directly activate UCP1, uncoupling respiration to make heat. Clinically, activating or recruiting BAT is of interest for combating obesity and type 2 diabetes by increasing energy expenditure. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->

17. With Δψ=ψmatrix−ψoutside=−140 mV and ΔpH=pHmatrix−pHoutside=1 at 310 K, calculate inward proton ΔG=FΔψ−2.303RTΔpH. Use F=96485 C/mol and R=8.314 J/mol/K.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Application).** Electrical work is −13.51 kJ/mol and chemical work −5.94 kJ/mol, totaling −19.45 kJ/mol (−201.5 mV equivalent). The pH coefficient at 37°C is 61.5 mV, not 59 mV. <!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
18. The malate-aspartate shuttle transfers NADH electrons from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix. Why is this shuttle needed, and why does a cell using the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle instead produce slightly less ATP? (Hint: which NADH-equivalent is delivered to which complex?)

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q18, Application).** Cytosolic NADH cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, so its electrons must be shuttled in. The malate-aspartate shuttle (liver, heart, kidney) regenerates matrix NADH, which enters at Complex I and yields ~2.5 ATP per electron pair. The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (skeletal muscle, brain) delivers electrons to ubiquinone via an FAD-linked enzyme, bypassing Complex I, so it yields only ~1.5 ATP per pair — hence ~30 ATP/glucose instead of ~32. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO9; bloom=Evaluate; difficulty=Synthesis; format=short-answer; minutes=8 -->
19. Cancer cells preferentially use aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) even when oxygen is available. Propose three mechanistic explanations for why rapidly proliferating cells would benefit from this apparently inefficient strategy.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Synthesis).** Three rationales: (1) speed — glycolytic ATP is produced far faster than by oxidative phosphorylation, matching the high demand of proliferation; (2) biosynthesis — diverting glucose carbon supplies precursors (G6P → ribose for nucleotides via the pentose phosphate pathway, 3-PG → serine, pyruvate → amino acids); (3) redox/NADPH and microenvironment — supports antioxidant defense and adapts to hypoxic, poorly vascularised tumor regions, while secreted lactate acidifies the milieu and favors invasion. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->

20. Name the four gluconeogenic bypass enzymes and count ATP, GTP and NADH consumed to make one glucose from two pyruvates.

22. Evaluate the efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation: how close is the actual ATP/O ratio to the theoretical maximum, and what factors cause inefficiency (proton leak, incomplete coupling)? What impact does membrane composition (cardiolipin content) have on coupling efficiency?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q22, Synthesis).** The measured P/O ratios (~2.5 ATP/NADH, ~1.5 ATP/FADH2) fall short of idealised maxima because of intrinsic proton leak, pump slip, and the H+ cost of importing Pi and ADP and exporting ATP. 23. Design an experiment to test whether a newly discovered mitochondrially targeted compound acts as an uncoupler, a chain blocker, or an ATP synthase inhibitor. What measurements (O₂ consumption rate, membrane potential, ATP levels, pH) would you take, and in what order?

<!-- SOLUTION

24. Cells can switch between aerobic respiration, fermentation, and fatty acid oxidation. 26. Mitochondria-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause oxidative stress. Evaluate the paradox that low ROS function in redox signaling (activating NF-κB, Nrf2, and HIF-1α) while high ROS cause macromolecular damage. What antioxidant systems exist in mitochondria (SOD2, thioredoxin, peroxiredoxin), and how might targeted mitochondrial antioxidants (MitoQ, SS-31) prevent disease?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q26, Synthesis).** ROS arise mainly from electron leak at Complexes I and III. At low levels they act as second messengers, reversibly oxidising thiols to activate redox-sensitive pathways (NF-κB, Nrf2, HIF-1α); at high levels they oxidise lipids, proteins, and mtDNA — a dose-dependent, hormetic relationship rather than a contradiction. Matrix-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ and SS-31 (elamipretide) concentrate where ROS are generated and may limit oxidative damage in ischemia-reperfusion and neurodegeneration without blunting physiological signaling. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.

30. Critically evaluate the "two-hit" model of mitochondrial disease: patients with heteroplasmic mtDNA mutations are often asymptomatic until a critical threshold (often ~80% mutated mtDNA) is exceeded. What cellular mechanisms (quality control, complementation in fused networks) maintain function below the threshold? At what threshold does bioenergetic failure occur, and how does tissue-specific metabolism (brain vs muscle vs liver) determine which tissue is most sensitive?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** Heteroplasmic cells tolerate mutant mtDNA because wild-type mitochondria and mtDNA-encoded subunits complement defective ones within the fused network, and quality-control fission/mitophagy removes the worst organelles — keeping bioenergetics adequate until mutant load passes a tissue-specific threshold (often ~70–90%, classically ~80%). Above threshold, oxidative phosphorylation fails and symptoms emerge. High-demand oxidative tissues (brain, then heart and skeletal muscle) cross the bioenergetic failure point first, whereas glycolytically flexible liver tolerates higher mutant loads, explaining the encephalo-myopathic presentation of many mtDNA diseases. See \cref{sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
