# Questions — Bacteria, Archaea, and Viruses {#sec:q_unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Classify a microbe or virus from evidence and predict a likely vulnerability.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Growth-rate, genome-size, and dilution calculations.
- **Assessment alignment:** Questions and Methods, Representing and Describing Data, Argumentation.
- **Misconception probe:** Viruses are not simply tiny bacteria; they use fundamentally different replication logic.
- **Transfer product:** Apply microbial diversity reasoning to antibiotics, biotechnology, ecology, and outbreaks.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

1. Give three representative chemical contrasts between bacterial and archaeal cell envelopes, distinguishing a typical example from a universal diagnostic rule.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** Three useful contrasts are: (1) many bacterial walls contain NAG–NAM peptidoglycan, whereas archaeal envelopes lack that bacterial polymer and can include S-layers, pseudomurein in selected methanogens, other polymers or no wall; (2) characteristic bacterial glycerophospholipids have ester-linked fatty acids on glycerol-3-phosphate, whereas characteristic archaeal lipids have ether-linked isoprenoids on glycerol-1-phosphate; (3) some archaeal lipids span the membrane as tetraether-rich architectures, while diether bilayers also occur. The third contrast is a possible architecture, not a claim that every archaeon has a monolayer or that any membrane cannot leak. Composition and experimental conditions determine thermal/permeability behavior, and classification needs multiple lines of evidence. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. What is the Gram stain? What does Gram-positive vs Gram-negative indicate about cell wall structure?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** The Gram stain (Hans Christian Gram, 1884) is a differential envelope-associated phenotype. In the typical Gram-positive pattern, a thick peptidoglycan wall retains the crystal-violet–iodine complex and appears purple. In the typical Gram-negative pattern, a thin peptidoglycan layer and outer membrane permit loss of the primary stain during decolorization, followed by a pink safranin counterstain. These usual patterns are not definitive species identification, proof of every envelope component or antibiotic susceptibility testing; Gram-variable organisms and preparation conditions limit inference. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. Describe the replication cycle of a bacteriophage. Distinguish lytic from lysogenic cycles.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** A bacteriophage attaches to a host receptor, injects its genome, and then follows one of two paths. In the lytic cycle the phage immediately hijacks host machinery to replicate its genome, assemble progeny, and lyse the cell, releasing new virions. In the lysogenic cycle the phage genome integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage and is replicated passively with the host until an induction signal switches it to the lytic cycle. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. What is a prophage? What signal triggers induction of the lytic cycle from lysogeny?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** A prophage is a bacteriophage genome that has integrated into the host bacterial chromosome (or persists as a plasmid) and is replicated along with the host during lysogeny. Induction to the lytic cycle is triggered by DNA-damaging stress (e.g., UV light or chemical mutagens) that activates the host SOS response: RecA stimulates autocleavage of the phage repressor (CI in lambda), de-repressing lytic genes. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

5. Place fosfomycin, D-cycloserine, bacitracin, vancomycin and beta-lactams on peptidoglycan synthesis.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** Fosfomycin inhibits MurA; D-cycloserine inhibits alanine racemase and D-Ala-D-Ala ligase; bacitracin interferes with carrier-lipid recycling; vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Ala precursors; beta-lactams inhibit PBP transpeptidation. Changing a target or precursor is not the same as hydrolyzing the drug. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Using *E. coli* as the specified example, describe binary fission. Under an idealized constant-generation-time model without death or resource limitation, how does doubling time relate to population growth? Why is this example not a universal bacterial genome/division inventory?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** In the stated *E. coli* example, a circular chromosome normally initiates bidirectional replication at *oriC*, the cell elongates and FtsZ-associated machinery coordinates septum formation before daughter cells separate. Other bacteria can have linear or multiple chromosomes and different division machinery; new mutations can also distinguish daughter genomes. For the stipulated ideal exponential model, $n=t/t_d$ and $N(t)=N_0 2^{t/t_d}$, so a shorter $t_d$ gives a larger growth rate. No claim of unlimited growth, universally identical daughters or identical bacterial chromosome inventories follows. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. Classify cocci in chains versus rods in pairs, and distinguish a phototroph from a chemolithoautotroph. Does morphology uniquely identify species?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** Cocci are spherical; rods are bacilli; arrangement describes chains or pairs, not species. A phototroph uses light energy; a chemolithoautotroph obtains energy/electrons from inorganic redox chemistry and carbon from CO2. Independent diagnostic or sequence evidence is needed for species identification. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. What is conjugation? What is an F plasmid?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Conjugation is horizontal gene transfer requiring direct cell-to-cell contact through a sex pilus. The F (fertility) plasmid encodes that pilus and the transfer machinery; an F+ donor transfers a copy of the F plasmid to an F− recipient. Because conjugative (R-) plasmids often carry antibiotic-resistance genes, conjugation is the principal route by which resistance spreads within and between bacterial species. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
9. Distinguish DNA viruses from RNA viruses. Give one example of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Recall).** DNA viruses store their genome as DNA and generally replicate using host (or viral) DNA polymerases with relatively high fidelity (e.g., herpesvirus, a dsDNA virus). RNA viruses store their genome as RNA and replicate with error-prone RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (or reverse transcriptase), giving high mutation rates and rapid evolution (e.g., influenza virus, a −ssRNA virus). See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO8; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. What is a retrovirus? Describe its replication cycle.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** A retrovirus is an enveloped +ssRNA virus (Baltimore Class VI, e.g., HIV) that carries reverse transcriptase. Its cycle: gp120 binds CD4 plus a coreceptor (CCR5/CXCR4) and the membrane fuses; reverse transcriptase copies the RNA genome into dsDNA; integrase splices that provirus into the host genome; host machinery transcribes and translates viral genes; new virions bud from the membrane and the protease matures them into infectious particles. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

11. A bacterial culture starts with 100 cells and has a doubling time of 20 minutes. Calculate: (a) the number of cells after 2 hours; (b) the approximate mass of bacteria (assuming 10⁻¹⁵ g/cell) after 24 hours. Why does exponential growth not continue indefinitely?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** With N(t)=100×2^(t/20 min), two hours gives six doublings and 6,400 cells. At 24 hours, 72 doublings give approximately 4.72×10^23 cells and, at the stipulated 10^-15 g/cell, 4.72×10^8 g. This unconstrained extrapolation is not a credible prediction for an ordinary finite culture: resource, oxygen, space and waste limits must be specified. The number alone, without a system-size/resource boundary, is not biologically impossible. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO10; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
12. Design a serial dilution experiment to count colonies from a bacterial sample expected to have ~10⁶ CFU/mL. Which dilutions would give countable plates (30–300 colonies)? Calculate CFU/mL from a plate showing 175 colonies at 10⁻⁵ dilution.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Prepare serial 10-fold dilutions (e.g., 10^-1 through 10^-7) and plate 0.1 mL of each in replicate. For ~10^6 CFU/mL the 10^-3 dilution gives 100 expected colonies in 0.1 mL; 10^-4 and 10^-5 give 10 and 1, below the stated countable range. CFU/mL = colonies ÷ (dilution factor × volume plated). For 175 colonies at 10^-5 plating 0.1 mL: 175 ÷ (10^-5 × 0.1) = 1.75×10^8 CFU/mL. If 1 mL was plated, the estimate is 1.75×10^7 CFU/mL. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

13. Compare the lytic and lysogenic cycles: what are the advantages of each for the phage? Under what environmental/physiological conditions does the lambda phage switch from lysogeny to the lytic cycle? (Name the specific protease.)

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q13, Application).** The lytic cycle maximizes immediate progeny when hosts are abundant; the lysogenic cycle preserves the genome through host scarcity or hostile conditions by hiding as a prophage. In lambda, the lysogeny-versus-lysis decision is governed by the CI repressor: DNA damage activates the host SOS response, RecA*-stimulated autocleavage destroys CI, de-repressing the lytic genes. The relevant phage protease activity is the RecA-coactivated autocleavage of the CI repressor. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

15. For specified archaeal examples, explain: (a) how ether-linked isoprenoids and membrane-spanning tetraether architecture can contribute to thermal/permeability adaptation, and why architecture alone does not prove membrane integrity at an arbitrary temperature; (b) how a salt-in halophile's protein surfaces can support function at high salt without treating acidic charge alone as a folding guarantee; (c) how a **hydrogenotrophic** methanogen couples H₂ oxidation to CO₂ reduction and energy conservation, distinguishing this route from every methanogen's substrate use.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** (a) Ether-linkage chemistry, membrane-spanning lipids, packing and composition can contribute to stability and reduced proton leak in a specified preparation. 16. The CRISPR-Cas system in bacteria provides adaptive immunity against phage. Explain: (a) spacer acquisition from phage DNA; (b) crRNA-guided Cas9 surveillance; (c) cleavage of matching invader DNA; (d) why the phage can evolve to escape CRISPR immunity (PAM sequence mutation). How did bacterial CRISPR inspire the molecular biology tool?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** (a) Adaptation: Cas1–Cas2 excise a protospacer from invading phage DNA and insert it as a new spacer into the CRISPR array. (b) Expression: the array is transcribed and processed into crRNAs that load Cas9 for surveillance. (c) Interference: crRNA base-pairs with matching phage DNA next to a PAM and Cas9 makes a double-strand cut, destroying the invader. (d) Phage escape by mutating the PAM or protospacer abolishes recognition. The programmable RNA-guided Cas9 nuclease was repurposed as a genome-editing tool. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

21. Evaluate the hypotheses for the origin of viruses: (a) regression hypothesis (viruses are degenerate cells); (b) escape hypothesis (mobile genetic elements that gained capsids); (c) primordial virion hypothesis (viruses predate cells). Which hypothesis is most consistent with current phylogenomic data (nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses and their relatives)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q21, Synthesis).** (a) The regression hypothesis holds that viruses are degenerate intracellular parasites that lost cellular machinery. (b) The escape hypothesis holds that viruses arose from cellular mobile genetic elements (plasmids/transposons) that acquired capsid genes. (c) The primordial-virion (virus-first) hypothesis holds that viruses predate cells. Current phylogenomic data — including nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (giant viruses) with translation-related genes and conserved capsid lineages — most strongly support a polyphyletic origin, with the virus-first/ancient-lineage view favored for the major capsid lineages; new conserved viral hallmark genes would change the conclusion. See \cref{sec:unit_VII_bacteria_archaea_viruses}.
SOLUTION -->

26. Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) in bacteria can be transduction, transformation, or conjugation.
