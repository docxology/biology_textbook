# Questions — Enzymes and the Kinetics of Catalysis {#sec:q_unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Infer Vmax, Km, or inhibition type from data and justify the mechanistic interpretation.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Michaelis-Menten, Lineweaver-Burk, and inhibition-pattern calculations.
- **Assessment alignment:** Concept Explanation, Statistical Tests and Data Analysis, Argumentation.
- **Misconception probe:** A catalyst changes rate, not the equilibrium constant or the sign of delta G.
- **Transfer product:** Apply enzyme-kinetic reasoning to drug dosing, metabolic control, or diagnostic assays.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

1. Define an enzyme. What type of macromolecule are most enzymes? Name one exception.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** An enzyme catalyzes a reaction by lowering its activation barrier without changing reaction equilibrium or being consumed overall. Most enzymes are proteins; ribozymes are catalytic RNA, including the ribosomal peptidyl-transferase center and self-splicing introns. Rate enhancement and dimensionful catalytic proficiency must not be interchanged.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. What is the active site? Explain the induced fit model.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** The active site comprises residues and spatial features involved in substrate binding and catalysis. Its extent depends on the enzyme and the definition used; no fixed surface percentage follows. In induced fit, ligand binding is coupled to a conformational change that can organize catalytic groups and alter solvent access. Hexokinase closure around glucose illustrates this coupling. Credit binding, catalysis and conformational response, not a universal site-size estimate. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. Distinguish Arrhenius activation energy Ea from activation free energy ΔG‡. How can an enzyme accelerate a reaction without altering its equilibrium ΔG?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** Arrhenius $E_a$ describes the temperature dependence of a rate constant through $k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$; activation free energy $\Delta G^{\ddagger}$ describes the free-energy barrier in transition-state theory. They are related but not interchangeable definitions. An enzyme supplies an alternative kinetic pathway through interactions such as acid–base, covalent or electrostatic catalysis. It accelerates approach to equilibrium in both directions without changing the free-energy difference between reactants and products or the equilibrium constant under the same conditions. Credit distinct barriers, a catalytic mechanism and unchanged equilibrium. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Define Michaelis–Menten Km and Vmax. Under what condition does Km approximate the binding dissociation constant?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** Vmax=kcat Etot and Km=(kminus1+kcat)/k1 for the simple scheme. Km is the substrate concentration giving half-maximal initial velocity; Kd=kminus1/k1. Only when kcat is much smaller than kminus1 does Km approximate Kd. Lower Km alone does not establish tighter equilibrium binding.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
5. Write the Michaelis-Menten equation. At $[\mathrm{S}]$ = Km, what fraction of Vmax is achieved?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Application).** The Michaelis-Menten equation is $v_0 = \dfrac{V_{max}\,[\mathrm{S}]}{K_m + [\mathrm{S}]}$. At **$[\mathrm{S}]$ = $K_m$**, substitute: $v_0 = \dfrac{V_{max}\,K_m}{K_m + K_m} = \dfrac{V_{max}\,K_m}{2K_m} = \dfrac{V_{max}}{2}$. So when the substrate concentration equals $K_m$, the enzyme runs at exactly **one-half of $V_{max}$** -- which is the operational definition of $K_m$ and a useful biological check that an assay is in the expected range. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Distinguish competitive, non-competitive, and uncompetitive inhibition. Which type changes Km? Which changes Vmax?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** In the specified reversible Michaelis–Menten models, competitive inhibition raises apparent Km without changing Vmax; pure noncompetitive inhibition lowers Vmax without changing Km; uncompetitive inhibition lowers both proportionally. In the simple binding schemes these correspond respectively to inhibitor binding free E, E and ES with equal affinity, or ES only. The kinetic pattern alone does not prove substrate resemblance or a physical active-site versus allosteric binding location. Credit all three parameter patterns and the model boundary. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is a coenzyme? Name two vitamin precursors and the coenzymes derived from them; distinguish recycling across a metabolic network from regeneration during one enzyme turnover.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** A coenzyme is an organic non-protein participant carrying electrons or chemical groups. Niacin is a precursor of NAD$^+$/NADH and riboflavin of FAD/FADH$_2$. A diffusible cosubstrate changes chemical form during turnover and can be regenerated by another reaction: glycolysis, for example, reduces NAD$^+$ to NADH rather than regenerating NAD$^+$ in the same step. A tightly bound cofactor may instead complete its redox cycle within an enzyme's overall catalytic cycle. Vitamin precursor, bound prosthetic group and diffusible cosubstrate are not interchangeable labels. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics,sec:unit_III_bioenergetics_and_respiration}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. Define allosteric regulation and distinguish it from cooperativity.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Allosteric regulation couples ligand binding at one site to activity or binding at another. Cooperativity concerns how occupancy changes subsequent binding or response. An allosterically regulated enzyme can retain a hyperbolic substrate curve; n=1 does not exclude allostery, and a sigmoid alone does not establish a specific oligomeric mechanism.
SOLUTION -->

9. Catalase is EC 1.11.1.6. Identify its broad EC class and explain why a pump classified EC 7 differs.

**Answer (Q9, Application).** EC 1 denotes oxidoreductases: catalase disproportionates peroxide in 2H2O2 to 2H2O+O2. 10. How does temperature affect enzyme activity? Name two effects of extreme temperatures on enzyme function.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Over a suitable temperature interval, increasing temperature can increase catalytic rate, as represented by an Arrhenius relation with positive Ea. Q10≈2 is an illustration, not a universal enzyme constant. At higher temperatures, reversible conformational changes and time-dependent inactivation can reduce measured activity; the apparent optimum depends on assay duration and conditions. Cooling generally slows turnover, but cold inactivation can occur in some enzymes. 11. An enzyme has Km = 2 mM and Vmax = 10 µmol/min. Calculate the reaction rate at $[\mathrm{S}]$ = 1 mM, 2 mM, and 10 mM. What does the saturation behavior tell you about enzyme kinetics?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** Model: $v_0 = \dfrac{V_{max}[\mathrm{S}]}{K_m + [\mathrm{S}]}$ with $K_m = 2$ mM, $V_{max} = 10$ µmol/min. **At $[\mathrm{S}]$ = 1 mM:** $v_0 = \dfrac{10 \times 1}{2 + 1} = \dfrac{10}{3} = \mathbf{3.33}$ µmol/min (33 % of $V_{max}$). **At $[\mathrm{S}]$ = 2 mM (= $K_m$):** $v_0 = \dfrac{10 \times 2}{2 + 2} = \mathbf{5.0}$ µmol/min (exactly 50 % of $V_{max}$). **At $[\mathrm{S}]$ = 10 mM:** $v_0 = \dfrac{10 \times 10}{2 + 10} = \dfrac{100}{12} = \mathbf{8.33}$ µmol/min (83 % of $V_{max}$). Interpretation: rate rises steeply when $[\mathrm{S}]$ < $K_m$ but flattens as $[\mathrm{S}]$ $\gg K_m$ -- the enzyme **saturates** because all active sites become occupied, so velocity asymptotically approaches $V_{max}$ and cannot exceed it no matter how much substrate is added. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->

12. In a competitive inhibition experiment, Km increases from 2 mM to 8 mM, but Vmax is unchanged (10 µmol/min). Calculate the inhibitor constant Ki if $[\mathrm{I}]$ = 50 µM (use the relationship K_m^app = Km(1 + $[\mathrm{I}]$/Ki)).

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Model for competitive inhibition: $K_m^{app} = K_m\left(1 + \dfrac{[\mathrm{I}]}{K_i}\right)$. Substitute $K_m = 2$ mM, $K_m^{app} = 8$ mM, $[\mathrm{I}]$ = 50 µM = 0.05 mM. $\dfrac{8}{2} = 1 + \dfrac{0.05}{K_i} \Rightarrow 4 = 1 + \dfrac{0.05}{K_i} \Rightarrow K_i = \dfrac{0.05}{3}=\mathbf{0.0167}\,\mathrm{mM}\approx\mathbf{17}\,\mu\mathrm{M}$. Check: $V_{max}$ is unchanged (10 µmol/min), consistent with competitive inhibition in the specified reversible model. High substrate approaches the same $V_{max}$ asymptotically; the kinetic result alone does not identify a physical binding site. The calculated $K_i$ is below the stated free inhibitor concentration. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->

18. Serine proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase) use the catalytic triad Ser-His-Asp. Explain the mechanism: how does the His residue act as a proton acceptor/donor, how does Ser become a nucleophile, and how does the oxyanion hole stabilize the tetrahedral intermediate?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q18, Application).** Serine proteases hydrolyse peptide bonds using the **Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad** and an oxyanion hole. Mechanism: (1) **Asp** orients and electrostatically stabilizes **His**; His acts as a **general base**, abstracting the proton from the Ser --OH, which converts Ser into a powerful **nucleophile**. (2) The Ser alkoxide attacks the substrate's carbonyl carbon, forming a negatively charged **tetrahedral intermediate**; the developing oxyanion is stabilized by H-bonds from backbone amides in the **oxyanion hole**, lowering $\Delta G^{\ddagger}$. (3) His donates its proton (now a general acid) to the leaving amine, releasing the first product and leaving a covalent **acyl-enzyme** intermediate. (4) Water, activated by His acting again as a general base, hydrolyses the acyl-enzyme through a second tetrahedral intermediate, releasing the carboxylic-acid product and regenerating free enzyme. His thus shuttles protons (base then acid), Ser provides the nucleophile, and the oxyanion hole stabilizes both tetrahedral transition states. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
SOLUTION -->

30. Criticise the following hypothesis: "The optimal enzyme evolves to have a Km equal to the in vivo substrate concentration." Using evolutionary arguments, real enzyme kinetic data, and the concept of "catalytic perfection" (kcat/Km approaching the diffusion limit ~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹), evaluate whether measured efficiency establishes an evolutionary optimum. Cite specific examples or a defined enzyme-survey comparison, and state the optimization objective and constraints.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** The quoted Km=$[\mathrm{S}]$ hypothesis is a claim to evaluate, not an established rule. In a simple Michaelis–Menten model, Km near $[\mathrm{S}]$ permits substantial concentration responsiveness, but this does not prove an optimization objective or exact equality. Bar-Even and colleagues' survey found generally moderate efficiencies; secondary-metabolism enzymes were on average about 30-fold slower than central-metabolism enzymes in that dataset, and substrate physicochemistry also constrained kinetic parameters \citep{bareven2011enzyme}. A high kcat/Km measures low-substrate catalytic efficiency, not organismal optimality. An objective such as maintaining a required pathway flux at low protein cost must also account for stability, substrate conditions and distributed flux control. Neither universal equality nor an equality restricted to strongly selected flux-controlling enzymes follows. Credit the defined survey comparison, explicit objective and rejection of the exact-equality inference. See \cref{sec:unit_I_enzymes_and_kinetics}.
