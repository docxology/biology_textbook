# Questions — Atoms, Molecules, and Chemical Bonds {#sec:q_unit_I_atoms_molecules .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Predict bond polarity, formal charge, or isotope behavior and connect it to a biological consequence.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Formal charge and electronegativity-difference reasoning.
- **Assessment alignment:** Concept Explanation, Statistical Tests and Data Analysis, Argumentation.
- **Misconception probe:** Weak interactions are not unimportant; many weak interactions together dominate structure and specificity.
- **Transfer product:** Use atomic reasoning to explain a medical tracer, enzyme cofactor, or membrane-solubility problem.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

1. Define an atom and identify its three subatomic particles. Which determines the element's identity?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element that retains the element's chemical properties. The three subatomic particles are **protons** (+1 charge, mass ≈ 1 Da, in the nucleus), **neutrons** (no charge, mass ≈ 1 Da, in the nucleus), and **electrons** (−1 charge, mass ≈ 0.0005 Da, in orbitals). The **atomic number** — the number of protons — defines the element. See \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. What is the atomic number of carbon? How many valence electrons does carbon have?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Carbon has atomic number **Z = 6** (six protons; six electrons when neutral). Its ground-state electron configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p², giving **four valence electrons** in the n = 2 shell. This tetravalence is the structural basis for carbon's central role in organic chemistry.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. Distinguish between covalent and ionic bonds. Give one biological example of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** A **covalent bond** is formed by shared electron pairs (ΔEN < ~1.7), e.g. the C–C and C–H bonds in glucose. An **ionic bond** results from full electron transfer (ΔEN > ~1.7) and the electrostatic attraction between resulting cations and anions, e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in physiological saline. Covalent bonds form the internal skeleton of biomolecules; ionic bonds tend to form between biomolecules and their ionic partners (Mg²⁺ stabilizing ATP phosphates; Ca²⁺ in calmodulin).
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Define electronegativity. Which element has the highest electronegativity?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** **Electronegativity** is the tendency of an atom in a bond to attract the shared bonding electrons toward itself. On the Pauling scale, **fluorine (F)** has the highest value, 3.98. Biologically most important: oxygen 3.44, nitrogen 3.04, chlorine 3.16, carbon 2.55, hydrogen 2.20. Differences in electronegativity across a bond create bond polarity and partial charges.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
5. What makes a covalent bond polar? What partial charges (δ+ and δ−) arise in an O–H bond?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** A covalent bond is **polar** when the two bonded atoms differ in electronegativity by ~0.4–1.7, so the shared electron density is unevenly distributed. In an **O–H bond** the electronegativity difference is 3.44 − 2.20 = 1.24; electrons spend more time near oxygen, giving O a partial negative charge (δ−) and H a partial positive charge (δ+). These partial charges underpin hydrogen bonding and water's solvent properties.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Define a hydrogen bond. Which atoms typically act as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** A **hydrogen bond** is an attractive, non-covalent interaction (~20 kJ mol⁻¹, about 1/20 the strength of a typical covalent bond) between a hydrogen atom that is covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (the **donor**) and a lone electron pair on another electronegative atom (the **acceptor**). In biology the donors and acceptors are almost always **N–H** or **O–H** on one side and **N** or **O** on the other. Hydrogen bonds stabilize DNA base-pairing, protein secondary structure, and the three-dimensional shape of nearly every aqueous biomolecule.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
7. What is a van der Waals interaction? In which large molecules are van der Waals forces collectively significant?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** **Van der Waals interactions** are weak (~1 kJ mol⁻¹ each), short-range attractions arising from transient dipoles induced by electron fluctuations (London dispersion). Individually negligible, they become collectively dominant in large hydrophobic surfaces — the interior of globular proteins, lipid-bilayer tails, and drug–protein binding pockets — where hundreds of contacts summate to many tens of kJ mol⁻¹.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO4; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
8. List four properties of water that arise directly from hydrogen bonding.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Four H-bond-derived properties of water: (1) an anomalously **high boiling point** (100 °C vs. −60 °C expected for H₂S); (2) **high specific heat** (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹) that buffers organismal temperature; (3) **high heat of vaporisation** (2257 J g⁻¹) enabling evaporative cooling; (4) **density maximum at 4 °C** — ice floats, insulating aquatic life. Other H-bond consequences: high surface tension, strong cohesion (xylem transport), and excellent solvation of polar solutes.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Apply; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
9. Define pH. In the ideal dilute-solution concentration approximation, what hydrogen-ion concentrations correspond to pH 7 and pH 4?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Application).** $\mathrm{pH}=-\log_{10}a_{\mathrm{H}^+}$. Under the stated approximation $a_{\mathrm{H}^+}\approx[\mathrm{H}^+]/(1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})$, pH 7 gives approximately $10^{-7}$ M=100 nM and pH 4 gives $10^{-4}$ M=100 µM. The activity ratio is 1,000; so is the approximate concentration ratio here. A one-unit pH decrease is a tenfold activity increase, not a clinical danger threshold \citep{buck2002phdefinition}. See \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. Define an acid and a base using the Brønsted-Lowry definition. Give one biological example of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** A Brønsted--Lowry acid donates a proton and a base accepts one. A protonated glutamate carboxyl group can donate H$^+$; unprotonated histidine imidazole can accept it. Protonation depends on pH and molecular environment. Conjugate pairs underlie buffering. In the physiological bicarbonate illustration, approximately 6.1 is an apparent pKa for the lumped dissolved-CO$_2$/bicarbonate system, not intact H$_2$CO$_3$ dissociation \citep{pines2016carbonicacid}. 11. Carbon forms four covalent bonds. Explain how this tetravalency allows carbon to form diverse structures including chains, branches, and rings. Why is carbon the basis of biological molecules rather than silicon?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** Carbon's valence permits four covalent bonds and catenation, supporting chains, branches and rings. Saturated tetrahedral carbon is commonly described by an sp$^3$ model with angles near 109.5 degrees; double-bond/aromatic carbon and triple-bond carbon require different geometries, not four equivalent tetrahedral bonds. Carbon can combine relatively persistent frameworks with functional groups that undergo selective, often enzyme-catalyzed transformations. Bond enthalpies alone do not determine reaction free energies, activation barriers or millisecond reaction rates. Silicon is also tetravalent, but its bond chemistry and the formation of extended silica under ordinary water-rich conditions impose different constraints on molecular diversity and cycling. These comparisons explain advantages of observed carbon-based aqueous biochemistry, not a proof about every conceivable non-terrestrial chemistry \citep{petkowski2020silicon}. See \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}.
SOLUTION -->

Polar groups can favor hydration, but total molecular structure and conditions determine solubility; a long nonpolar region can outweigh an alcohol's hydroxyl contribution. 14. Explain cholesterol's low aqueous solubility and favorable partitioning into lipid environments. How do its hydroxyl group and nonpolar surface contribute, and why must both enthalpy and entropy be considered?

**Answer (Q14, Application).** Cholesterol's hydroxyl group can hydrogen-bond with water, but its large nonpolar ring-and-tail surface favors partitioning into lipid environments. 19. The peptide bond in proteins involves C–N with partial double-bond character due to resonance. Explain how this resonance prevents rotation around the peptide bond and what structural constraint this places on protein backbone geometry.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q19, Application).** The peptide C--N bond is a **resonance hybrid**: structure I (C=O, C--N single, lone pair on N) and structure II (C--O$^-$, C=N$^+$ double, lone pair delocalized into the carbonyl $\pi$ system). The hybrid gives the C--N bond ~40 % double-bond character, with an intermediate length of ~132 pm (between 147 pm single and ~127 pm double). Because rotating about a partial double bond would require breaking $\pi$ overlap (~80 kJ/mol barrier), the six atoms of the peptide unit (C$_\alpha$--C(=O)--N(H)--C$_\alpha$) are locked **coplanar** and almost always **trans** (cis is ~8 kJ/mol higher, except before proline). The structural consequence: only the $\varphi$ (N--C$_\alpha$) and $\psi$ (C$_\alpha$--C) torsions can vary, sharply restricting backbone conformational space and making regular $\alpha$-helices and $\beta$-sheets possible. See \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}.
SOLUTION -->

20. Magnesium (Mg²⁺) is a common enzyme cofactor. Using your understanding of atomic structure, explain why Mg²⁺ can stabilize negatively charged phosphate groups in ATP. What orbital property of Mg²⁺ makes it a Lewis acid?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q20, Application).** Mg²⁺ has electron configuration [Ne] after losing both 3s electrons — its **empty 3s/3p valence shell** makes it an archetypal **Lewis acid** (electron-pair acceptor). Consequences: (i) the small, doubly-charged cation has a high **charge density (q/r ≈ 2.8 e/Å)**, generating a strong electrostatic field that neutralizes the triply-negative phosphate chain of ATP⁴⁻; the biologically active species is actually **Mg·ATP²⁻**, not free ATP⁴⁻. (ii) By coordinating with the β- and γ-phosphate oxygens, Mg²⁺ **polarizes the terminal P–O bond** and orients the γ-phosphate for nucleophilic attack — kinases are typically **100–1000× slower** in the absence of Mg²⁺ (K_m for Mg²⁺ ≈ 0.1–1 mM). (iii) In ribosomes and ribozymes, Mg²⁺ similarly stabilizes RNA folding and activates the 2′-OH for phosphodiester attack. Physiological free [Mg²⁺] ≈ 0.5 mM, total ≈ 20 mM (mostly bound) — tight regulation, because even small shifts alter every ATP-dependent reaction. See \cref{sec:unit_I_atoms_molecules}.
SOLUTION -->

<!-- SOLUTION

30. Propose a molecular mechanism by which a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor (e.g., vorinostat) could be used to treat cancer. Trace from HDAC inhibition → altered histone acetylation state → chromatin opening → transcription of tumor suppressor genes → reduced tumor cell survival. Identify the specific bond type removed/restored at each step.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q30, Synthesis).** **Hypothesis:** inhibiting HDAC with vorinostat raises histone acetylation, opens chromatin, and re-expresses silenced tumor-suppressor genes, reducing tumor-cell survival. **Step-by-step bond chemistry:** (1) HDAC normally hydrolyses the **amide (isopeptide) bond** of acetyl-lysine, removing the acetyl group and regenerating a positively charged Lys --NH$_3^+$; vorinostat's hydroxamate chelates the active-site Zn$^{2+}$, blocking this hydrolysis.
