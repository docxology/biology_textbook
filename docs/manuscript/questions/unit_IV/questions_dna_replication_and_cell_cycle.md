# Questions — DNA Replication and the Cell Cycle {#sec:q_unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle .unnumbered}


## Instructor Use and Coverage Notes {.unnumbered}

- **Coverage target:** Calculate replication timing or error burden and infer checkpoint consequences.
- **Model/data emphasis:** Replication-fork speed, error-rate, and cell-cycle timing calculations.
- **Assessment alignment:** Concept Explanation, Questions and Methods, Argumentation.
- **Misconception probe:** High fidelity is not automatic; it depends on overlapping safeguards whose roles can differ by cell-cycle phase, rather than universally interchangeable fail-safes.
- **Transfer product:** Transfer replication logic to cancer, aging, viral replication, or antibiotic targets.
- **Grading focus:** award full credit for mechanism, evidence, boundary conditions, and units when a calculation is required.
- **Suggested use:** draw one recall item, one application item, and one synthesis item when building a short quiz from this bank.

<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
1. Describe the semiconservative model of DNA replication. What experiment confirmed it (Meselson-Stahl, 1958)?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q1, Recall).** Each daughter molecule retains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand, so replication is semiconservative. Meselson and Stahl (1958) grew E. coli in heavy $^{15}$N medium, switched to light $^{14}$N, and used CsCl density-gradient centrifugation: after one round all DNA was hybrid (intermediate) density, and after two rounds half was hybrid and half fully light, which is exactly what the semiconservative model predicts and rules out conservative and dispersive models. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
2. List the enzymes required for DNA replication and state one function of each.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q2, Recall).** Helicase unwinds the double helix at the fork; single-strand binding (SSB) proteins keep the strands apart; topoisomerase/gyrase relieves the supercoiling ahead of the fork; primase lays down RNA primers; DNA polymerase (Pol III in bacteria; Pol δ/ε in eukaryotes) extends DNA and proofreads; a sliding clamp (β-clamp/PCNA) confers processivity; and DNA ligase seals the nicks between fragments. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
3. Why do the conventional replicative DNA polymerases considered here require a primer 3′-OH? Which enzyme supplies the RNA primer?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q3, Recall).** Conventional replicative DNA polymerases extend a pre-existing primer 3′-OH rather than initiate a strand de novo. Primase synthesizes the RNA primer (in the bacterial primosome or eukaryotic Pol α–primase complex). This describes the machinery considered here, not every DNA-synthesizing enzyme or protein-primed initiation mechanism. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO2; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
4. Distinguish the leading strand from the lagging strand during replication. Why does the lagging strand form Okazaki fragments?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q4, Recall).** The leading strand is synthesized continuously in the 5'→3' direction as the fork opens, while the lagging strand runs antiparallel so its 5'→3' synthesis proceeds away from the fork. Because polymerase cannot synthesize 3'→5', the lagging strand is made in short Okazaki fragments (~1,000–2,000 nt in prokaryotes, ~100–200 nt in eukaryotes), each separately primed and later joined by ligase. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO1; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=3 -->
5. Describe the antiparallel, complementary organization of double-stranded DNA. How does nucleotide sequence store information, and why does base complementarity support copying and repair?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q5, Recall).** The paired strands have covalent sugar–phosphate backbones running in opposite 5′→3′ directions. In canonical Watson–Crick pairing A pairs with T and G with C; information is encoded by base order, not by a different backbone chemistry for each message. An intact strand specifies a complementary sequence, providing a template for polymerases and a reference for some repair pathways. DNA's deoxyribose lacks RNA's 2′-OH, reducing susceptibility to the intramolecular backbone cleavage it enables; paired strands also provide sequence redundancy. These features support storage and copying but do not make DNA damage-free or ensure that every lesion is repairable. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO6; bloom=Remember; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
6. Name the four phases of the eukaryotic cell cycle. What occurs in each?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q6, Recall).** The four phases are G1 (cell growth and preparation for DNA synthesis), S (DNA replication, producing identical sister chromatids), G2 (growth and checking of replicated DNA before division), and M (mitosis plus cytokinesis, segregating chromosomes into two daughter cells). G1, S, and G2 together form interphase. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->

7. What are cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs)? Give one example of a cyclin-CDK pair and its function.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q7, Recall).** Cyclins are regulatory proteins whose abundance changes through the cycle. Canonical cell-cycle CDKs such as CDK1 require cyclin association and appropriate phosphorylation/inhibitor regulation for normal activity; cyclin binding alone is not a complete activation rule for every CDK-family member. Cyclin B–CDK1 promotes mitotic entry by phosphorylating targets including nuclear lamins and condensin regulators. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->

8. What is a checkpoint? Name three checkpoints in the cell cycle and their functions.

**Answer (Q8, Recall).** Checkpoint signaling can delay progression in response to particular damage, replication or attachment signals; passage does not certify an error-free genome. G1/S DNA-damage control can delay S-phase entry; G2/M control can delay mitosis after damage or incomplete replication; unattached kinetochores promote spindle-checkpoint inhibition of anaphase. 9. In the canonical damage-response model, compare an ATM-null human cell with an otherwise matched ATM-proficient cell after a double-strand break is introduced in G1. Assume p53, p21 and ATR remain functional. Which upcoming cell-cycle transition is expected to show weakened damage-induced delay first, and why does ATM loss not imply loss of every checkpoint or every p53 response?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q9, Application).** The next transition is G1/S: loss of ATM weakens the rapid DSB-triggered ATM–CHK2/p53 response and p21-dependent CDK inhibition, so damage-induced delay before S phase is impaired \citep{khanna1995checkpoint}. Functional p53 does not compensate automatically for a missing upstream signal, but ATR and other signaling routes can retain or later induce responses, especially when resection or replication produces ssDNA. ATM loss therefore does not abolish all p53 activity, the ATR-dependent replication-stress response or the spindle checkpoint. The prediction is conditional on the stipulated G1 starting state; without cell-cycle position there is no universal “first checkpoint.” Award the G1/S prediction, the ATM-to-p53/p21 mechanism and the remaining-pathway distinction. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO5; bloom=Understand; difficulty=Recall; format=short-answer; minutes=2 -->
10. What is telomerase? Why is its activity low or undetectable in many differentiated human somatic cells, and why does that not establish a universal telomere-loss rate or division limit?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q10, Recall).** Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein reverse transcriptase that uses its RNA template to extend chromosome 3′ ends with TTAGGG repeats. Many differentiated human somatic cells repress hTERT, limiting telomere maintenance and contributing to a tumor-suppressive proliferation barrier; some somatic stem and activated immune cells retain activity. Net shortening depends on cell type, culture conditions, oxidative damage and end processing, not a fixed loss per division. Dysfunction at particular short or unprotected ends can activate senescence pathways; neither mean length nor telomerase repression specifies one universal division count. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.

## Questions 11–20: Mechanisms and evidence {.unnumbered}

11. Using the Meselson-Stahl data: if ¹⁵N/¹⁵N-labeled DNA is allowed to replicate for two rounds in ¹⁴N medium, what densities of DNA are expected and in what proportions? Show your reasoning with a diagram.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q11, Application).** After two rounds of replication in $^{14}$N medium the original two $^{15}$N strands are conserved, each now paired with a new light strand, giving two hybrid (intermediate-density) molecules. The other two molecules are fully light ($^{14}$N/$^{14}$N). So the expectation is a 1:1 ratio of intermediate to light density and no fully heavy DNA, which is the diagnostic signature of semiconservative replication. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->
<!-- assess: LO=LO3; bloom=Analyze; difficulty=Application; format=short-answer; minutes=5 -->
12. Compare the E. coli Pol III–β-clamp machinery with the eukaryotic Pol δ/ε–PCNA machinery. How does each sliding clamp increase processivity, what differs between the clamps and polymerase assignments, and why does processivity matter for copying a large genome?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q12, Application).** Both clamps encircle DNA and tether polymerases, reducing dissociation and increasing nucleotides copied per binding event; processivity is distinct from the nucleotide-addition rate. The E. coli β clamp is a homodimer supporting Pol III on both strands, whereas eukaryotic PCNA is a homotrimer supporting the usual Pol ε leading-strand and Pol δ lagging-strand assignments. Clamp loaders use ATP to load the rings. Efficient copying of long templates depends on this shared strategy, while a large nuclear genome also uses many origins rather than one polymerase copying it uninterrupted. Award the shared mechanism and real bacterial/eukaryotic contrasts, not an unsupported universal processivity fold increase. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->

13. Explain how eukaryotic Pol δ strand displacement, RNase H/FEN1 processing and DNA ligase I complete Okazaki fragments. Why is primer removal not a primase function?

Pol δ extends DNA and displaces downstream primer-containing flaps; RNase H and FEN1 process RNA/flaps, then ligase I seals remaining nicks. Award synthesis versus removal, flap processing and ligation; the exact contributions depend on substrate context. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.

15. Aphidicolin inhibits replicative polymerases α, δ and ε; hydroxyurea (HU) reduces dNTP supply through ribonucleotide-reductase inhibition. For cells already in S phase, compare moderate conditions that slow but permit ongoing DNA synthesis with severe, prolonged inhibition. Discuss fork speed, reversible slowing/stalling versus collapse, ATR signaling versus full CHK1 activation, and cell-cycle progression. Which measurements would be needed before assigning either drug a fixed arrest phase?

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q15, Application).** Both perturbations can slow forks; sufficient inhibition can stall them, but drug identity alone does not specify speed or replisome integrity. Moderately slowed forks may remain protected and restart after washout. ATR outputs and CHK1 activation depend on stress and cellular context. Cells treated during S phase may accumulate within S under strong inhibition; a fixed G1/S boundary arrest cannot be inferred from a drug name. Award the two biochemical targets, conditional fork/checkpoint outcomes and proposed measurements: DNA-fiber synthesis/restart, break assays, ATR/CHK1 readouts and time-resolved DNA content with synthesis labeling. 16. In an artificial linear telomere model, assume an initial tracked length of 15 kb, a constant loss of 100 bp per division, and a stipulated stopping threshold of 6 kb. Calculate divisions to that threshold. Explain why this is not a general human-cell lifespan prediction, and distinguish checkpoint-associated senescence from crisis after proliferation barriers are bypassed.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q16, Application).** The stipulated reserve is 15 kb − 6 kb = 9 kb = 9,000 bp, so 9,000 bp/(100 bp/division) = 90 divisions to the model threshold. Both the constant rate and 6-kb stopping rule are artificial assumptions, not universal measurements. Real outcomes depend on the distribution of individual telomere lengths, end protection, cell state and environment; population doublings also need not equal each individual cell's division history. Dysfunctional ends can activate arrest or senescence when barriers remain intact. Continued proliferation after those barriers are bypassed can lead to crisis with fusions, breakage–fusion–bridge cycles and extensive cell death; a rare surviving clone may acquire telomere maintenance through telomerase or ALT. Reaching the model threshold alone does not establish crisis. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.

17. Unattached kinetochores promote spindle assembly checkpoint signaling. Explain formation of the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC), inhibition of APC/C–Cdc20, and the subsequent separase/cohesin transition when checkpoint signaling falls. Distinguish low tension from absence of attachment.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q17, Application).** Unattached kinetochores recruit MAD/BUB proteins that promote MCC formation (Mad2–BubR1–Bub3–Cdc20), inhibiting APC/C–Cdc20 and delaying securin/cyclin B destruction. 18. For an idealized diploid cell with $2n=4$ that undergoes one S phase followed by meiosis I and II, track chromosome and chromatid counts in each resulting cell. Explain how homolog separation, sister separation, crossing over and independent assortment can contribute to haploid, genetically variable products. Assume no nondisjunction or chromosome loss.

<!-- SOLUTION
**Answer (Q18, Application).** After S phase the cell contains four replicated chromosomes (eight chromatids); count chromosomes by centromeres. Meiosis I separates the two homologs of each pair, leaving two cells with two replicated chromosomes (four chromatids) apiece. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, yielding four haploid products, each with two single-chromatid chromosomes (two DNA molecules). Prophase-I crossing over exchanges aligned DNA between nonsister chromatids of homologs, reshuffling existing sequence variants without requiring a new mutation. Independent orientation of the two homolog pairs at metaphase I permits $2^2=4$ parental-origin chromosome sets across many meioses when crossovers are ignored, not four guaranteed distinct products from one meiosis. With identical sisters and no crossing over, a single meiosis produces two complementary chromosome sets, each represented twice. The idealized four products need not be genetically distinct or all become functional gametes. See \cref{sec:unit_IV_dna_replication_and_cell_cycle}.
SOLUTION -->

20. In a metazoan cell-cycle model, geminin inhibits Cdt1 and contributes to preventing origin relicensing.
