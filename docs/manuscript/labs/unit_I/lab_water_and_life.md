2. Apply hydrogen-bonding reasoning to explain why ice is less dense than liquid water and what that anomaly means for organisms living in a temperate lake during winter.
3. Compare cohesion (water-to-water hydrogen bonding) and adhesion (water-to-other-surface attraction). Use the contrast to explain how the cohesion–tension model lifts a column of water from soil to the canopy of a tall tree.

## Lab Context: Water — The Molecule of Life {.unnumbered}

Water's extraordinary properties arise from its bent molecular geometry (104.5° H–O–H bond angle) and the resulting network of hydrogen bonds. Each water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds — two through lone pairs (O as H-bond acceptor) and two through O–H groups (H-bond donor). This network gives water its high specific heat (4.18 J/g·°C), high surface tension (72 mN/m), strong cohesion/adhesion, and the ability to dissolve ionic and polar solutes. Most of these properties are exploited by living organisms.

In this lab you will analyze heat-absorption data for water and oil, interpret capillary-rise diagrams, and track osmotic movement in a model membrane dataset. The required work is paper-based so the scientific reasoning is centered on controls, units, gradients, and reproducible interpretation.

### Worked Example: Temperature rise of a desert organism using q = m c ΔT {.unnumbered}

**Problem:** Two illustrative 2.0 kg organisms absorb net 840 J, one 70% water and one 40% water by mass. Use c_water=4.18 and c_other=1.50 J g^-1 K^-1 for both.

**Solution:** At 70% water, C=1400(4.18)+600(1.50)=6752 J/K, so deltaT=840/6752=0.1244 K. At 40%, C=800(4.18)+1200(1.50)=5144 J/K, giving 0.1633 K. The comparison uses the same mixture model, not water-only heat capacity in just one organism. These are net stored heat inputs; real trajectories also depend on changing heat losses and physiology.


### Source-Governance Checkpoint {.unnumbered}

**Printed packet W1 — synthetic teaching data, not empirical measurements.** No animal or human measurements are represented.

**Thermal cards:** Equal masses 100 g, net power 10 W, initial 20.0 degrees C; ideal c_water=4.18 and c_oil=2.00 J g^-1 K^-1. At times 0,2,4,6,8,10 min, modeled water temperatures are 20.00,22.87,25.74,28.61,31.48,34.35; oil temperatures 20,26,32,38,44,50. Each is one ideal trajectory, not six replicates. Thermometer resolution is 0.1 K. A no-heater control stays at 20.0 throughout; equal net input and mass are required for c=Q/(m deltaT).

**Capillary cards W2:** Water gamma=0.072 N/m, rho=1000 kg/m^3, contact angle 0; oil gamma=0.032 N/m, rho=900 kg/m^3, angle 0; g=9.81 m/s^2. Use h=2gamma cos(angle)/(rho g r). Printed vertical-scale coordinates are tube bottom y=0 mm, reservoir y=10 mm, water meniscus y=39.4 mm and oil meniscus y=24.5 mm, with r=0.50 mm. Thus heights above reservoir are 29.4 and 14.5 mm (drawing resolution 1 mm). Water r=1.00 mm has height 14.7 mm. The xylem schematic is soil reservoir -> narrow cohesive water column -> evaporating leaf meniscus; label adhesion at walls and tension transmitted from leaf. This is diagram evidence, not a claim that passive capillarity alone lifts tall-tree sap.

**Osmosis cards W3:** Three model bags per medium begin at 10.00 g. Membrane passes water but not the supplied sucrose, with zero initial pressure difference. Internal concentration is 0.20 M; external hypo=0.10 M, iso=0.20 M, hyper=0.30 M. At times 0,5,10,20 min, mean masses are hypo 10.00,10.40,10.70,11.00; iso 10.00,10.00,10.00,10.00; hyper 10.00,9.60,9.30,9.00 g. At every nonzero time the three synthetic bag masses are mean-0.02, mean, mean+0.02 g; balance resolution 0.01 g. Timepoints within a bag are repeated observations, not independent replicates.

**Instructor key:** Water gains 2.87 K per 2 min versus oil 6 K; c estimates are 4.18 and 2.00. Capillary height halves on doubling radius. At 20 min the mass changes are +1,0,-1 g (ranges 0.04 g); isotonic bags are the negative control. Initially water flows into bags in hypo and out in hyper; pressure and dilution change the later gradient. For the 70% versus 55% synthesis at 4.2 kJ, capacities are 6752 and 5948 J/K, so rises are 0.622 and 0.706 K. No thermoregulatory threshold is asserted without species evidence. Governance: authored ideal-model packet W1–W3; revise if net heat loss, contact angle, solute permeability or pressure differs.

## Analysis Questions {.unnumbered}

1. Which substance heated more quickly — water or oil? How does this reflect differences in specific heat, and what is the ecological significance for oceans moderating coastal climates?
2. Why does the modeled water column rise higher than the oil column? Separate liquid–wall adhesion from liquid–liquid cohesion, and use surface tension, density, contact angle and radius in W2 rather than calling both interactions adhesive forces.
3. Interpret the printed xylem schematic rather than claiming to have observed celery. Distinguish evaporation-generated tension from adhesion/cohesion and from the passive capillary-height calculation.
4. For each W3 medium, report the model bag's 20-minute mass change and initial water-potential gradient. Explain why an isotonic mean does not mean water molecules stop crossing the membrane.
5. Patients receiving intravenous fluids receive 0.9% NaCl (normal saline) rather than pure water. Predict what would happen if red blood cells were placed in pure water vs 3% NaCl solution. Explain using water potential ($\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p$).

## Post-Lab Synthesis {.unnumbered}

> **Concept Check (Synthesis):** A comparative physiologist studies two related lizards. Lizard X lives in a wet temperate habitat and has body water content of about 70% by mass; lizard Y lives in a hot, arid environment and has body water content of about 55%. Daytime ambient heat loads on both animals are similar in magnitude.
>
> (a) Predict, using the q = m c ΔT framework from the worked example, how the two lizards' core-temperature trajectories would differ over a one-hour basking interval if other heat-exchange routes (conduction, evaporation) are held constant. Compute the expected temperature rise for a representative heat load of 4.2 kJ deposited in each animal (use 2 kg body mass for both), and interpret the biological consequence for homeostasis.
> (b) Evaluate the trade-off between water content and thermal buffering. Identify one biological cost of a high-water tissue strategy (for example, dehydration risk during prolonged heat exposure) and one cost of the low-water strategy. Explain why each animal's strategy can still be adaptive in its own habitat without claiming one strategy dominates the other.
> (c) Design a paper-data extension comparing the same mixtures across 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 kJ net stored heat. Hold body mass and heat-capacity assumptions fixed. Compare each predicted difference with a stipulated 0.1 K measurement resolution; distinguish detectability from a biological thermoregulatory threshold, which this packet does not supply.
